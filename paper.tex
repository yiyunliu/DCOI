\section{Introduction}

Dependency-tracking type systems govern how functions can depend on their
inputs. Essentially, these systems prevent the flow of information from
high-level sources to low-level results.

The \emph{Dependent Calculus of Indistinguishability (DCOI)}~\cite{liu:dcoi}
adapts dependency tracking to the context of a dependently-typed programming
language. The primary goal of dependency tracking in DCOI is to identify
irrelevant arguments. These arguments can be erased during compilation to
produce faster execution and can be ignored during equivalence checking. A key
notion is \emph{indistinguishability}: a definition of program equivalence
that takes into account the parts of the program that an observer may depend
on. However, dependency-tracking type systems serve many purposes, such as
staged compilation and information flow control~\cite{abadi:dcc}.

Prior work lays the foundation for the design of a dependently-typed
programming language based dependency tracking, called DCOI. DCOI's conversion
may use indistinguishability at any observer level when comparing types for
equality. Despite this coarse notion of equality, prior work showed that DCOI
is type sound.

However, prior work focused on syntactic methods for reasoning about
DCOI. While sufficient for type soundness, they cannot be used to identify a
logically consistent fragment of DCOI that enjoys properties such as weak
normalization and decidable observer-indexed equivalence. For flexibility,
\dcoi is designed around a \emph{Pure Type System} (PTS), where various
instances of sorts, rules and axioms determine the versions of quantification
that is allowed in the language. While some instances of PTSs are known to
allow nontermination (such as when including the $[[Type]]:[[Type]]$ axiom),
others (such as those that include a predicative universe hierarchy, or a
single impredicative sort) can form the basis of a consistent logic.

In this paper, we define the language \name, which is a dependent type theory
featuring a predicative universe hierarchy. This calculus retains many of the
features of \dcoi, including level-indexed dependent function types
($[[Pi x : l A . B]]$), level-indexed dependent pairs ($[[Sig x : l A. B]]$),
level-indexed propositional equality $[[a ~l b]]$, and an empty type
$[[bot]]$.

\section{\name}

In this section, we present the grammar and judgements of \name,
which is an instantiation of the \longname (\dcoi)~\citep{liu:dcoi} with
the Pure Type System (PTS)~\citep{barendregt:PTS} axioms and rules
of a predicative universe hierarchy.
The grammar is given in \cref{fig:grammar},
which is parameterized over a meet-semilattice of dependency levels
(following \citet{abadi:dcc}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{alignat*}{2}
  [[G]], [[D]] & \Coloneqq [[nil]] \mid [[G ++ x : l A]] & \quad & \text{Typing contexts with levels and types} \\
  [[E]], [[F]] & \Coloneqq [[nil]] \mid [[E ++ x : l]] & \quad & \text{Grading contexts with levels} \\
  [[A]], [[B]], [[C]], &&& \\
  [[a]], [[b]], [[c]], [[p]] & \Coloneqq & \quad & \text{Terms} \\
  & \mid [[x]] \mid [[Univ i]] \mid [[Void]] \mid [[absurd b]] & \quad & \textit{variables, type universes, empty type, absurd} \\
  & \mid [[Pi x : l A . B]] \mid [[\ l x . b]] \mid [[b l a]] & \quad & \textit{function types, abstractions, applications} \\
  & \mid [[Sig x : l A . B]] \mid [[(l a, b)]] \mid [[let (l0 x, y) = l a in b]] & \quad & \textit{pair types, pairs, matching} \\
  & \mid [[a ~ l b : A]] \mid [[refl]] \mid [[J c l p]] & \quad & \textit{equality types, reflexivity, J eliminator}
\end{alignat*}
\caption{Grammar of contexts and terms}
\label{fig:grammar}
\end{figure}

We use the metavariable $[[l]]$ for dependency levels,
and $[[l0 /\ l1]]$ for the meet of $[[l0]]$ and $[[l1]]$.
As in the previous section, we use the low $[[L]]$ and high $[[H]]$ levels in our examples,
with $[[L < H]]$.
Similarly, we use the metavariables $[[i]], [[j]], [[k]]$ for universe levels,
which we take to be meta-level naturals,
and $[[max i j]]$ for the maximum of $[[i]]$ and $[[j]]$.
Although our mechanization uses de Bruijn indices for representing binding
and simultaneous substitutions,
for clarity we use named variables and single substitutions for our presentation.
We use the metavariables $[[x]], [[y]], [[z]]$ for variables
and $[[a { b / x }]]$ for the substitution of a term $[[b]]$ in place of the variable $[[x]]$
inside the term $[[a]]$.

The main judgement is the typing judgement,
which has the form \judgement{$[[G |- a : l A]]$} for a term $[[a]]$
that is well type under context $[[G]]$ at observer level $[[l]]$
with type $[[A]]$.
To reduce clutter,
we use the judgement \judgement{$[[G |- a : A]]$} to mean that there exists some level $[[l]]$
such that $[[G |- a : l A]]$ holds.
The typing rules are defined mutually with the context well-formedness rules,
both given in \cref{fig:typing:dep}.
When viewed as a PTS, the \rref{Wt-Univ,Wt-Pi} correspond
to the sort axioms $([[Univ i]], [[Univ i + 1]])$ for every $[[i]]$
and the sort rules $([[Univ i]], [[Univ j]], [[Univ max i j]])$ for every $[[i]], [[j]]$.

  \drules[Wt]{$[[G |- a : l A]]$}{Well-typedness}{Var,Univ,Empty,Absurd,Pi,Abs,App}
  \caption{Well-formedness and typing rules (universes, empty, functions)}
  \label{fig:typing:dep}
\end{figure}

Deferring our discussion of using levels for dependency tracking to \cref{sec:dep},
the remaining rules for variables, function abstractions, function applications,
the empty type, and its eliminator are standard for dependent type theory.
To change the type of a term to an equivalent one,
typing uses a definitional equality that is defined in terms of indistinguishability (\cref{sec:indist}).
We then look at the rules for dependent pair types (\cref{sec:pairs}),
and finally for propositional equality types (\cref{sec:eq}),
which internalize indistinguishability.

\subsection{Dependency tracking} \label{sec:dep}

The observer level $[[l]]$ of a typing judgement $[[G |- a : l A]]$
tracks what level variables $[[a]]$ may use, as well as the level at which $[[a]]$ may be used.
The former is enforced by the level constraint premise in \rref{Wt-Var},
which permits a variable to be used only if the observer level is at least as high as that of the variable.
Informally, higher-level information cannot leak into a lower-level term.

In \rref{Wt-Abs}, there is no restriction on the level of the function parameter,
so a function may be applied to an argument of a higher level,
so long as the annotated level of the function parameter
matches the annotated level of the application in \rref{Wt-App}.
Although the function cannot directly use a higher-level variable in its lower-level body,
allowing a higher-level parameter is still useful because it may be used in the function's \emph{type},
which is typed at an arbitrary level independent of the function itself
in the second premise of \rref{Wt-Abs}.
By \rref{Wt-Pi}, there are no restrictions on the levels of the domain and codomain types either
(as long as they match).

As an example, consider the following typing judgement,
using $[[l A -> B]]$ as shorthand for nondependent functions $[[Pi x : l A . B]]$
where $[[x]]$ does not appear in $[[B]]$.
%
\begin{displaymath}
  [[:concrete: nil |- (\ H X . \ H x . \ L f . f H x) : L (Pi X : H Univ 0 . H X -> L (H X -> X) -> X)]]
\end{displaymath}

The entire function itself is typed at level $[[L]]$,
while the parameters $[[X]]$ and $[[x]]$ are annotated at level $[[H]]$,
so the inner function body cannot be $[[x]]$,
for instance, even if it has the correct type.
On the other hand, the parameter $[[f]]$ is a function that takes a high-level $[[X]]$
and returns a low-level $[[X]]$, so $[[f H x]]$ is a valid function body.
Meanwhile, the type of the entire function itself need only be well-typed at \emph{some} level,
and in particular is well-typed at $[[H]]$,
which would allow using the high-level $[[X]]$
in the codomain of the type.

In general, we can prove a regularity lemma stating that the type of a well-typed term
is itself well typed at a level not necessarily related to that of the term,
and may even be higher, as is the case for the example above.
Intuitively, this is permitted because there is no way to retrieve the type of a term at run time,
so the type of a lower-level term can safely use higher-level information
without it leaking into the term.

\begin{lemma}[Regularity]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{Wt\_Wff}, \suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{Wt\_regularity}}
  \label{lem:regularity}
  If $[[G |- a : l A]]$, then $[[|- G]]$ holds,
  and there exist some $[[l0]], [[i]]$
  such that $[[G |- A : l0 Univ i]]$.
\end{lemma}

While generally a lower-level term may not meaningfully use higher-level information,
a proof of the empty type $[[Void]]$ at \emph{any} level
may be eliminated to \emph{any other} level via \rref{Wt-Absurd}.
By logical consistency, which we prove in \cref{sec:semmeta},
there is no closed term of type $[[Void]]$,
and so it cannot contain any meaningful information,
and furthermore represents a dead execution path.
From a type-theoretic perspective,
the unconstrained elimination level allows us to handle impossible cases of a lower-level term
using a higher-level proof of its impossibility.

\subsection{Definitional equality and indistinguishability} \label{sec:indist}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \drule{Wt-Conv} % maybe this should go back in the previous figure
  \drule{E-Conv}
  \drule{P-AppAbs}
  \vspace{0.5\baselineskip}
  \caption{Conversion, definitional equality, and parallel reduction ($\beta$ only) rules}
  \label{fig:conv}

The typing rules also include \rref{Wt-Conv}, shown in \cref{fig:conv},
wich allows converting the type of a well-typed term to a definitionally equal type.
Our definitional equality is untyped, but eventually relies on the levels of variables,
so its judgement form \judgement{$[[E |- a == b]]$} requires a grading context $[[E]]$.
In \rref{Wt-Conv}, this is provided by the erasure operation \judgement{$[[| G |]]$},
which denotes dropping all type annotations to convert a typing context into a grading context.

Definitional equality is defined by \rref{E-Conv} in \cref{fig:conv},
which separately incorporates parallel reduction and indistinguishability.
For convenience, we define the shorthand \judgement{$[[E |- a <=> b @ l]]$}
as a level-annotated form of definitional equality:
it holds iff $[[a =>* c0]]$, $[[b =>* c1]]$, and $[[E |- c0 == c1 @ l]]$.
Consequently, $[[E |- a == b]]$ holds iff there exists some level $[[l]]$
such that $[[E |- a <=> b @ l]]$ holds.

Parallel reduction \judgement{$[[a => b]]$} consists of the $\beta$-reduction rules for our elimination forms,
listed in \cref{fig:conv} and in following subsections,
along with rules for the reflexive, congruent closure, omitted here.
Multi-step parallel reduction \judgement{$[[a =>* b]]$} is then the transitive closure of parallel reduction.
This formulation of equality is in contrast to the system by \citet{liu:dcoi},
where parallel reduction is merely a metatheoretic tool for proving type safety.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \drules[I]{$[[E |- a == b @ l]]$}{Indistinguishability}{Var,Univ,Empty,Absurd,Pi,Abs,App}
  \drules[GI]{$[[E |- a == b @ l l0]]$}{Guarded indistinguishability}{Dist,Indist}    
  \caption{(Guarded) indistinguishability rules (universes, empty, functions)}
  \label{fig:indist}
\end{figure}

Indistinguishability is mutually defined with guarded indistinguishability,
given in \cref{fig:indist}.
Guarded indistinguishability is used in \rref{I-App};
otherwise, two terms are indistinguishable at observer level $[[l]]$
if they have the same shape and their subterms are also indistinguishable at $[[l]]$.

We say that indistinguishability is \emph{coarser} than mere $\alpha$-equivalence
(and hence $[[E |- a == b]]$ is coarser than mere $\beta$-equivalence of $[[a]]$ and $[[b]]$)
because more terms may be indistinguishable while not necessarily being $\alpha$-equivalent.
This is because the use of guarded indistinguishability when comparing arguments in \rref{I-App},
which are labelled at $[[l0]]$, but observed at $[[l]]$.
If $[[l0 <= l]]$, meaning that the observer can fully see the arguments,
then \rref{GI-Dist} applies, and the applications are indistinguishable
if both the functions and the arguments are indistinguishable at $[[l]]$ respectively.
Otherwise, if $[[not (l0 <= l)]]$, then \rref{GI-Indist} applies,
and the arguments are too high to be observed at $[[l]]$,
so indistinguishability of the applications does not consider the arguments.
As a simple example,
$[[:concrete: (# w : L ++ x : H #) ++ y : H |- (\ H z . w) H x == (\ H z . w) H y @ L]]$
holds even though $[[x]]$ and $[[y]]$ could be anything,
because the observer level is $[[L]]$,
while the arguments' levels are $[[H]]$ and therefore unobservable.
Indistinguishability of unobservable arguments articulates the intuition that
functions may safely ignore these arguments that they cannot meaningfully use.

A level comparison also occurs in \rref{I-Var},
where a variable may only be indistinguishable from itself
if the observer level is high enough to observe it.
This prevents equating arbitrary terms via function applications, such as
$[[:concrete: x : H ++ y : H |- (\ H z . z) H x == (\ H z . z) H y]]$,
since the bodies of the functions are not indistinguishable.
Without this level comparison,
we have via \rref{E-Conv} the equalities $[[nil |- Unit == (\ H z . z) H Unit]]$,
$[[nil |- (\ H z . z) H Unit == (\ H z . z) H Void]]$,
and $[[nil |- (\ H z . z) H Void == Void]]$,
which would permit a closed term of $[[Void]]$ via repeated applications of \rref{Wt-Conv}.

An important property of indistinguishability is the simulation property,
which enforces that indistinguishable terms must reduce in lockstep.
This is crucial for proving further syntactic lemmas in \cref{sec:synmeta},
such as the preservation of definitional equality between two terms as they reduce.

\begin{lemma}[Simulation]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{simulation\_star}}
  \label{lem:simulation}
  If $[[E |- a0 == b0 @ l]]$ and $[[a0 =>* a1]]$,
  then there exists some $[[b1]]$ such that $[[b0 =>* b1]]$
  and $[[E |- a1 == b1 @ l]]$.
\end{lemma}

To ensure that simulation holds,
additional level constraints must be enforced when typing
the eliminators for pairs and equality proofs, as we will see in the next two subsections.
We explain in further detail how omitting these constraints violate simulation in \cref{sec:scrutinee-level}.

\subsection{Propositional equality} \label{sec:eq}

\begin{figure}[h]

The propositional equality type internalizes the indistinguishability judgement,
enabling reasoning about indistinguishability within \name itself.
The typing rules for constructs related to the equality type are given in \cref{fig:typing:eq}.
An equality type $[[a ~ l0 b : A]]$ represents the proposition that $[[a]]$ and $[[b]]$
are indistinguishable by observers at level $[[l0]]$.
By \rref{Wt-Eq}, the endpoints must themselves be well typed at level $[[l0]]$,
since it only makes sense to talk about the indistinguishability of observable terms,
just as \rref{I-Var} only holds for observable variables.
The observer level $[[l0]]$ must also be bounded by the overall level $[[l]]$
to maintain logical consistency,
which we explain in detail in \cref{sec:eq-level}.
Its constructor is the usual reflexivity proof $[[refl]]$ for well-typed terms,
given by \rref{Wt-Refl}.

In \rref{Wt-J}, the eliminator $\ottkw{J}$ takes as target
an equality proof $[[p]]$ annotated at level $[[l2]]$,
along with a method $[[c]]$.
The target must be well typed as an equality between $[[a]]$ and $[[b]]$
of type $[[A]]$, observed at level $[[l0]]$.
The observer, so to speak, is the motive $[[C]]$,
abstracted over the right-hand size of the equality $[[x]]$
(the left-hand side being fixed at $[[a]]$)
and the equality proof itself $[[y]]$.
As the observer, the motive must be well typed at level $[[l0]]$.
The type of the method is the motive instantiated with $[[a]]$ and $[[refl]]$,
while the type of the fully-applied eliminator is the motive instantiated with $[[b]]$ and $[[p]]$.
The levels of $[[x]]$ and $[[y]]$ must therefore match the levels of $[[b]]$ and $[[p]]$, respectively.
Because by \rref{P-JRefl} the eliminator reduces on $[[refl]]$ to the method,
the level of the method must match the level $[[l]]$ of the overall eliminator.
The level of the target is not exactly $[[l]]$ but is bounded by it;
its justification is a subtle technical issue,
which we defer to \cref{sec:scrutinee-level}.

In addition to requiring the well-typedness of the equality proof $[[p]]$,
\rref{Wt-J} also takes as premises the well-typedness
of the endpoints $[[a]], [[b]]$ of the equality and of their type $[[A]]$.
These premises are required to strengthen the induction hypotheses
when we later prove the \nameref{thm:fundamental} for our semantic metatheory.
However, these premises can be omitted by applying \nameref{lem:regularity}
and inversion to the well-typedness of $[[p]]$.
We present this simplification as an admissible
\rref{Wt-J-alt}\punctstack{,}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{T\_J\_simpler}}
using a double horizontal bar to indicate admissibility,
in \cref{fig:typing-eq-lemmas}.

\section{Syntactic metatheory} \label{sec:synmeta}

In this section, we develop the syntactic metatheory of \name,
ultimately culminating in subjection reduction,
\ie type preservation of typing with respect to parallel reduction.
The metatheory has been mechanized in Coq,
and the development can be found in \repo{} under the \texttt{proofs} directory.
Definitions, lemmas, and theorems given here are annotated with a footnote

also need to carry around some well-gradedness information.
The rules for the judgement can be seen as the same as the typing rules,
but with all typing information stripped out, leaving only the levels,

Indistinguishability is reflexive for well-graded terms

Parallel reduction and its transitive closure admit the substitution lemmas
\rref*{P-subst}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Par\_cong}} and
\rref*{Ps-subst}\punctstack{,}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Pars\_cong\_star}} respectively.
An additional useful lemma is that single parallel reduction itself is already reflexive
(\rref*{P-refl}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Par\_refl}}).
These are given in \cref{fig:pars-lemmas}.

The key lemmas of parallel reduction are the diamond property and confluence,
which state that if a term reduces to two different terms,
those two terms themselves must converge back to the same term.
The proof uses the notion of complete development%
\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{tstar}}
by \citet{takahashi:par}.

\begin{lemma}[Diamond]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Par\_confluent}}
  \label{lem:diamond}
  If $[[a => b0]]$ and $[[a => b1]]$, then
  there exists some $[[c]]$ such that $[[b0 => c]]$ and $[[b1 => c]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Confluence]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Pars\_confluent}}
  \label{lem:confluence}
  If $[[a =>* b0]]$ and $[[a =>* b1]]$, then
  there exists some $[[c]]$ such that $[[b0 =>* c]]$ and $[[b1 =>* c]]$.
\end{lemma}

Confluence is required to show that
reduction of definitionally equal terms preserves definitional equality,
since definitional equality is defined in \rref{E-Conv}
as the indistinguishability of the two terms after parallel reduction.
The proof relies on the \nameref{lem:simulation} property of indistinguishability
to reconnect the two sides after reduction.

\begin{lemma}[Preservation of definitional equality]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{iconv\_par2}}
  Suppose $[[E |- a0 == b0]]$.
  If $[[a0 => a1]]$ and $[[b0 => b1]]$,
  then $[[E |- a1 == b1]]$.
\end{lemma}

As with indistinguishability,
definitional equality admits weakening
(\rref*{E-weak}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_renaming}}),
and is reflexive for well-graded terms
(\rref*{E-refl}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_refl}}),
symmetric (\rref*{E-sym}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_sym}}),
and transitive (\rref*{E-trans}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_trans}}),
stated in \cref{fig:defeq-lemmas}.
Inverting the premises of transitivity,
each of them independently have some level at which two terms are indistinguishable,
so combining them requires the more general transitivity of \rref*{I-trans}.
The proof also relies on simulation.

\begin{lemma}[Injectivity of function types]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_pi\_inj}}
  If $[[E |- Pi x : l0 A0 . B0 == Pi x : l1 A1 . B1]]$
  then $[[l0]] = [[l1]]$, $[[E |- A0 == A1]]$,
  and $[[E ++ x : l0 |- B0 == B1]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Injectivity of pair types]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_sig\_inj}}
  If $[[E |- Sig x : l0 A0 . B0 == Sig x : l1 A1 . B1]]$
  then $[[l0]] = [[l1]]$, $[[E |- A0 == A1]]$,
  and $[[E ++ x : l0 |- B0 == B1]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Injectivity of equality types]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_eq\_inj}}
  If $[[E |- a0 ~ l0 b0 : A0 == a1 ~ l1 b1 : A1]]$
  then $[[l0]] = [[l1]]$,
  and there exists an $[[l >= l0]]$
  such that $[[E |- a0 <=> a1 @ l]]$ and $[[E |- b0 <=> b1 @ l]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Injectivity of universes]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_univ\_inj}}
  If $[[E |- Univ i == Univ j]]$ then $[[i]] = [[j]]$.
\end{lemma}

    [[p =>* refl]] \wedge \exists a', b', [[a =>* a']] \wedge [[b =>* b']] \wedge [[E |- a == b @ l0]]
\end{align*}

In this section, we use a logical predicate to show weak
normalization and consistency for \name.
We structure the section as follows. In Section~\ref{sec:logpred}, we
present the definition of the logical predicate and its properties. In
Section~\ref{sec:soundness}, we define valid substitutions and
semantic typing judgments based on the logical predicate and then
prove the fundamental theorem, which states that all syntactically
well-typed terms must be semantically well-typed, from which we derive
weak normalization and consistency. In Section~\ref{sec:decconv}, we combine
weak normalization and the standardization theorem to recover a decidable
type conversion algorithm.
\subsection{A logical predicate for \name}
\label{sec:logpred}
To characterize semantically well-behaved terms through the logical
predicate, we need to first define the notion of neutral and normal terms.

% the notion of neutral and normal terms so we can characterize
% semantically well-behaved terms.

% logical predicate, we need to first define normal and neutarl forms

Figure~\ref{fig:normalform} defines neutral and normal forms in the
form of two judgments \judgement{$[[ne a]]$} and \judgement{$[[nf a]]$}.
These predicates characterize a set of terms that are free of redexes.
This property can be formulated using parallel reduction as follows.
\begin{lemma}
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{nf\_refl}}
  If $[[nf a]]$ or $[[ne a]]$, then for all $[[b]]$ such that $[[a =>
  b]]$, we have $[[a]] = [[b]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}[Terms that are weakly normalizing]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{wn}, \suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{wne}}
  Given a term $[[a]]$, if there exists some term $[[b]]$ such that
  $[[a =>* b]]$, then we say that $[[a]]$ weakly normalizes to a
  normal (resp. neutral) form, which we denote in judgment form as
  \judgement{$[[wnf a]]$} (resp. \judgement{$[[wne a]]$}).
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{fig:logpred} gives the full definition of our logical
predicate.
Similar to \citet{abel:irrelevance}'s semantic interpretation of types, 
we define our logical predicate, which takes the form
\judgement{$\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$}, as an inductive relation
recursively defined over the universe level $[[i]]$. The
metavariable $S$ represents a family of terms indexed by levels. 
We use the notation \judgement{$\member{a}{[[l]]}{S}$} to indicate that $[[a]]$ is
a term that belongs to the set in $[[S]]$ that is indexed by $[[l]]$.
When constructing indexed sets, we use the notation
\judgement{$\buildset{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{\ldots}$} to indicate a family of sets
parameterized by $[[l]]$.
The judgment $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ reads that under the
grading context $[[E]]$ and universe level $[[i]]$, the type $[[A]]$
can be interpreted as the family of sets $[[S]]$.

Unlike logical relations for simply typed languages, the logical
predicate for \name cannot be defined as a total function since not
all terms can be assigned a meaning. Instead, the logical predicate
$\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ simultaneously specifies the
semantically well-formed types $[[A]]$ and its meaning $[[S]]$. %  A key
% result we will present later in this section is the functionality of
% the logical predicate; that is, each term can only correspond to one
% interpretation $[[S]]$ if there exists one.

For concision, we introduce the notion of semantic inhabitance to
avoid mentioning the side condition that a type is well-formed when we
want to talk about whether a term inhabits the set obtained from
interpreting the type.
\begin{definition}[Semantic inhabitance]
  Given a context $[[E]]$ and a level $[[l]]$, 
  we say that $[[a]]$ \emph{semantically inhabits} the type $[[A]]$
  at level $[[l]]$ if there exists some $[[i]]$ and $[[S]]$ such that
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $\member{a}{[[l]]}{S}$.
\end{definition}

In \rref{SWt-Pi,SWt-Sigma}, the
metavariable $R$ represents a relation between lambda terms and the
indexed family of terms $S$. Ignoring the conclusion of the rules, 
\rref*{SWt-Pi} and \rref*{SWt-Sigma} share the same preconditions,
which ensure their well-formedness in the following sense:
for every term $[[a]]$ that semantically inhabits the type $[[A]]$ at
level $[[l0]]$, the type $[[B {a / x}]]$ is well-formed. % This
% The interpreted sets appearing in the conclusion follows our usual
% intuition. 
In the function case, the definition of $\prodspace$
specifies the semantic inhabitants of a function type as the set of
terms that maps well-behaved terms from the input type to well-behaved
output type.

The logical predicate takes in the grading context $[[E]]$ as a
parameter to ensure that the $[[S]]$ only includes terms that are
well-graded with respect to the indexed level.
This property is
formally stated as follows and can be proven by straightforward
induction over the logical predicate.
\begin{lemma}[Escape]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUniv\_Ok}}
  \label{lem:escape}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $\member{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{S}$,
  then $[[E |- a : l]]$.
\end{lemma}
We refer to \cref{lem:escape} as the escape lemma as it is
reminiscent of the escape lemmas found in \citet{abel:irrelevance,
adjedj:mltt-coq}, though instead of escaping from the
logical predicate to the syntactic typing judgment, we only require
the much weaker well-gradedness judgment. The well-gradedness
restriction will be necessary for us to show that indistinguishable
types have the same meaning.

Similar to its syntactic counterpart, the logical predicate satisfies
the subsumption property.
\begin{lemma}[Subsumption for the logical predicate]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_subsumption}}
  Suppose $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
  If $[[l0 <= l1]]$ and $\member{[[a]]}{[[l0]]}{S}$,
  then $\member{[[a]]}{[[l1]]}{S}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate by induction over the derivation and \rref{Wg-sub}.
\end{proof}

Next, we show some properties which will be useful in
\cref{sec:soundness} for the proof of the fundamental

when presenting the proof sketches.

The logical predicate satisfies the following inversion principles. 
These inversion principles are not immediately obtainable by inverting
the derivation because we close our interpretation with parallel
reduction, a full
reduction relation, in \rref{SWt-Step}. For brevity, we only show the cases
for equality, function, and empty types.
\begin{lemma}[Inversion of the logical predicate (selected)]
  \label{lem:interpinv}\leavevmode
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_Eq\_inv}}
    If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[a ~ l0 b : A]]}{S}$, then $S =
    \buildset{[[p]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- p : l]] \wedge (\eqspace([[p]],
      [[l0]], [[a]], [[b]], [[E]]) \vee [[wne p]])}$
  \item \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_Fun\_inv}}
    If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[Pi x : l0 A . B]]}{S}$, then there
    exists $S_A$ and $R$ such that following holds:
    \begin{itemize}
    \item $S = \buildset{[[b]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- b : l]] \wedge \prodspace([[b]], [[l]], [[l0]], S_A, R)}$
    \item $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_A}$
    \item $\forall a, \member{a}{[[l0]]}{S_A} \to \exists S_B, R(a,
      S_B)$
    \item $\forall a, S_B, \member{a}{[[l0]]}{S_A} \to R(a, S_B) \to
      \interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[B {a / x}]]}{S_B}$
    \end{itemize}
  \item \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_Void\_inv}}
    If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[Void]]}{S}$, then $S =
    \buildset{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- a : l]] \wedge [[wne a]]}$
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate by induction over the derivation of the logical
  predicate.
\end{proof}

The set of terms that semantically inhabit a type are closed by
expansion (i.e. backward reduction).
\begin{lemma}[Backward closure]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_back\_clos}}
  \label{lem:backclos}
  Suppose $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $[[E |- a : l]]$.
  If $[[a => b]]$ and $\member{[[b]]}{[[l]]}{S}$,
  then $\member{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{S}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  By induction over the derivation of the logical predicate. The
  \rref*{SWt-Univ} case requires \rref*{SWt-Step}, the backward
  closure property baked into the definition of the logical
  predicate. All other cases are trivial.
\end{proof}

The logical predicate satisfies cumulativity; if a type has an
interpretation at a lower level, then it must also have the same
interpretation at a higher level.
\begin{lemma}[Cumulativity]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_cumulative}}
  \label{lem:cumulativity}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $[[i]] \leq [[j]]$, then
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[j]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  By induction on the derivation of $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$
  and the transitivity of $\leq$ for universe levels.
\end{proof}

\Rref{SWt-Step} closes the interpretation of types under
expansion. We can also show that the meaning of types is
preserved under forward reduction through the following lemma.
\begin{lemma}[Reduction preserves meaning]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_preservation}}
\label{lem:lrpreservation}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $[[A => B]]$, then
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  By induction over the derivation of
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
  \Rref{SWt-Step} is the only interesting case, where we
  leverage the diamond property (\ref{lem:diamond}) of
  parallel reduction to reconcile two potentially distinct reductions
  from the type $[[A]]$.
\end{proof}


Next, we prove that the logical predicate is functional. That is,
given a context $[[E]]$ and a universe level $[[i]]$, a type $[[A]]$
can correspond to at most one interpretation $[[S]]$.
\begin{lemma}[Functionality]
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_deterministic}}
\label{lem:lrfunc}
If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_0}$ and
$\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_1}$, then $S_0 = S_1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  By induction over the derivation of
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_0}$.
  The \rref*{SWt-Step} case is immediate by \cref{lem:lrpreservation}.
  For all other cases, the conclusion
  follows by applying the inversion
  properties (\ref{lem:interpinv}) on
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_1}$.
\end{proof}

The logical predicate is functional not only for types that are
syntactically equal, but also for types that are
indistinguishable. This property is needed to fully justify the
soundness of the type conversion rule since the type conversion
involves both reduction and indistinguishability.
\begin{lemma}[Functionality for indistinguishable types]
\label{lem:lrfunci}
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_IEq}}
If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_0}$,
$\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[B]]}{S_1}$, and
$[[E |- A == B @ l ]]$ for some $[[l]]$,
then $S_0 = S_1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  The argument is similar to the one for Lemma~\ref{lem:lrfunc}.
  We start by induction over the derivation of
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_0}$.
  For case \rref*{SWt-Step}, we need both Lemma~\ref{lem:lrpreservation}
  and simulation (\ref{lem:simulation}).
  For other cases, we apply the inversion properties on
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[B]]}{S_1}$.

  The function and sigma cases both require one extra step to complete the
  proof. Consider the case where $[[A]] = [[Pi x : l0 A0 . B0]]$ and
  $[[B]] = [[Pi x : l0 A1 . B1]]$. The
  induction hypothesis says that for every term $[[a]]$ that
  semantically inhabits $[[A0]]$, if a type $[[C]]$ is
  indistinguishable from $[[B0 {a / x}]]$, then $[[C]]$ and $[[B0 {a /
  x}]]$ have the same meaning. To complete the proof, we need to
  leverage the induction hypothesis by instantiating the variable
  $[[C]]$ with the type $[[B1 { a / x}]]$ and prove that $[[B0 {a /
    x}]]$ is indistinguishable from $[[B1 {a / x}]]$.  From the
  premise that $[[Pi x : l0 A0 . B0]]$ and $[[Pi x : l0 A1 . B1]]$ are
  indistinguishable, we already know that $[[B0]]$ and $[[B1]]$ are
  indistinguishable.

  To prove that $[[B0 {a / x}]]$ and $[[B1 {a / x}]]$ are
  indistinguishable, we want to apply
  the substitution rule for indistinguishability
  (\rref{I-subst}), which requires $[[a]]$ to be well-graded at $[[l0]]$.
  By escape (\ref{lem:escape}), we finish the
  proof by deducing the well-gradedness of $[[a]]$ from the fact that
  $[[a]]$ semantically inhabits the input type $[[A0]]$.
\end{proof}

property, which establishes the connection between the inhabitants of
the logical predicate and terms that weakly normalizes to normal or

Before we state and prove adequacy, we first show some
useful facts about normal forms. Following \citet{geuvers:cc-sn}, we
introduce a dummy constant $[[dummy]]$ to the set of untyped lambda
terms for presentation purposes. The dummy term satisfies the rules
specified in Figure~\ref{fig:dummy}.

Reductions and normal forms are preserved when we undo the substitutions
involving $[[dummy]]$.
\begin{lemma}[Antirenaming for Par]
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{Par\_antirenaming}}
  \label{lem:paranti}
  If $[[a {dummy / x} => b]]$, then there exists some term $[[b0]]$
  such that $[[b]] = [[b0 {dummy / x}]]$ and $[[a => b0]]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}[Antirenaming for normal and neutral forms]
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{ne\_nf\_renaming\_with\_d}}
  \label{lem:nfneanti}
  If $[[nf a {dummy / x}]]$ (resp. $[[ne a {dummy / x}]]$), then $[[nf
  a]]$ (resp. $[[ne a]]$).
\end{lemma}
From Lemmas~\ref{lem:paranti} and \ref{lem:nfneanti}, we derive the
following corollary about weak normalization.
\begin{lemma}[Antirenaming for weak normalization]
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{wn\_antirenaming}}
  \label{lem:wnanti}
  If $[[wnf a {dummy / x}]]$, then $[[wnf a]]$.
\end{lemma}

In \citet{geuvers:cc-sn}, the $[[dummy]]$ constant can be defined as a
syntax sugar for a fresh variable. However, this is not possible in \name
because we want \rref{I-D, Wg-D} to hold. When the grading context is empty,
there is no variable we are allowed to pick. However, since
\rref{I-Absurd} does not impose any scope checking on the scrutinee of
the empty eliminator, we can define $[[dummy]]$ as $[[absurd x]]$ for
an arbitrarily picked variable $[[x]]$. It is easy to verify that this
definition respect the rules in Figure~\ref{fig:dummy} and the
antirenaming lemmas.

Finally, we state and prove the adequacy lemma.
\begin{lemma}[Adequacy]
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{adequacy}}
  \label{lem:adequacy}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$, then the following holds:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item If $\member{a}{[[l]]}{S}$, then $[[wnf a]]$
  \item If $[[E |- a : l]]$ and $[[wne a]]$, then $\member{a}{[[l]]}{S}$
  \item $[[wnf A]]$
  \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  By induction over the derivation of
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
  The only interesting part of the proof is showing normalization for
  well-formed function and sigma
  types. We focus on the function type and the proof for sigma type is
  almost identical.

  In the function case, we have $[[A]] = [[Pi x : l0 A0 . B0]]$. By
  the induction hypothesis, we already know $[[wnf A0]]$ and for all
  terms $[[a]]$ that semantically inhabits $[[A0]]$, the term $[[B0 {a
    / x}]]$ is weakly normalizing. To show that $[[Pi x : l0 A0. B0]]$
  is weakly normalizing, it suffices to show that $[[B0]]$ is weakly
  normalizing. 

  Since the induction hypothesis about $[[B0]]$ is universally
  quantified, we need to find a concrete term to instantiate $[[a]]$
  with. By the induction hypothesis about $[[A0]]$, we also know that
  if $[[E |- a : l]]$ and $[[a]]$ is a neutral term, then $[[a]]$
  semantically inhabits $[[A0]]$. By \rref{I-D}, we pick $[[dummy]]$
  for $[[a]]$ and deduce that $[[B0 {dummy / x}]]$ is weakly
  normalizing. By Lemma~\ref{lem:wnanti}, we conclude that $[[B0]]$ is
  also weakly normalizing.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Soundness}
\label{sec:soundness}
To define semantic well-typedness, we need to first define the notion
of valid substitutions, sometimes also referred to as valuations.
While we continue to use named variables rather than de Bruijn indices,
% to bring the presentation closer to the mechanization,
in this section we frame substitution in terms of \emph{substitution maps}
(or simply \emph{substitutions} for short)
and simultaneous substitutions.

\begin{definition}[Substitution maps]
  A substitution map $\rho$ is a mapping from variables to terms.
  We use $\dom(\rho)$~to denote the variables in the domain of $\rho$,
  $\rho(x)$~the term to which $\rho$ maps $[[x]]$,
  $[[nil]]$~the identity substitution map,
  $[[(rho ++ { a / x })]]$~the extension of $[[rho]]$
  by a mapping from $[[x]]$ to $[[a]]$,
  and $[[a { rho }]]$~the simultaneous substitution
  of the variables $x \in \dom(\rho)$ in $[[a]]$ by $\rho(x)$.
\end{definition}
The singleton substitution map $[[{ a / x }]]$ is then defined as $[[(nil ++ { a / x })]]$,
the single extension of the identity substitution map.

\begin{definition}[Valid Substitutions]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok}}
  \label{def:semwf}
  A substitution map is a valid substitution 
  from $[[G]]$ to $[[D]]$,
  written \judgement{$\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$},
  if for every $[[x : l A in G]]$,
  $[[| D | |- rho(x) : l]]$ holds,
  and for all $i$, $S$ such that
  $\interp{[[|D|]]}{[[i]]}{[[A { rho }]]}{S}$, 
  we have $\member{\rho(x)}{[[l]]}{S}$.
  % Intuitively, $\rho$ replaces the variables listed by $[[G]]$,
  % and the terms $\rho$ replaces them by have free variables in $[[D]]$.
\end{definition}

By definition, every term $[[rho( x)]]$ in a valid substitution map
$[[rho]]$ from $[[G]]$ to $[[D]]$ is well-graded with respect to the erased context
$[[|D|]]$. This allows us to derive the following lemma about
well-gradedness and valid substitutions.
\begin{lemma}[Valid substitutions for well-graded terms]
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{iok\_$\rho$\_ok\_morphing}}
  If $[[|G| |- a : l]]$ and $\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$, then $[[|D| |-
  a{rho} : l ]]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate by iterating the substitution principle for well-gradedness (\rref*{Wg-subst}).
\end{proof}

Valid substitutions satisfy the following admissible structural rules.
\begin{lemma}[Structural rules for valid substitutions]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok\_id}, \suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok\_cons}}
  \begin{mathpar}
    \mprset{fraction={===}}
    \infer[\ottdrulename{V\_id}]{~}{\sws{[[G]]}{[[nil]]}{[[G]]}} \and
    \infer[\ottdrulename{V\_cons}]{
      \interp{[[|D|]]}{[[i]]}{[[A { rho }]]}{S} \\
      \member{[[a]]}{[[l0]]}{S} \\
      \sws{[[D]]}{[[rho]]}{[[G]]}
      }{ \sws{[[D]]}{[[rho ++ {a / x} ]]}{[[G ++ x : l0 A ]]}    }
  \end{mathpar}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \Rref{V-id} is easily justified by the part of the adequacy lemma
  that states well-graded neutral terms (variables in $[[G]]$ in
  this case) semantically inhabit any well-formed types.

  \Rref{V-cons} requires functionality (\ref{lem:lrfunc}) and
  cumulativity (\ref{lem:cumulativity}) to convert the fact that
  $\member{[[a]]}{[[l0]]}{S}$ for the specific $S$ from the
  precondition to the universal statement that
  $\member{[[a]]}{[[l0]]}{S_0}$ for arbitrary $j$ and $S_0$ such that
  $\interp{[[|D|]]}{[[j]]}{[[A { rho }]]}{S_0}$, as needed by the
  definition of valid substitutions.
\end{proof}

Next, we define semantic typing based on the definitions of valid
substitutions and the logical predicate.
\begin{definition}[Semantic well-typedness]
  \label{def:semwt}
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{SemWt}}
  A term $[[a]]$ is semantically well typed with type $[[A]]$
  at level $[[l]]$ under context $[[G]]$,
  written \judgement{$\swt{[[G]]}{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[A]]}$},
  if for every $\rho$, $[[D]]$,
  given $\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$,
  the term $[[a {rho}]]$ semantically inhabits the type $[[A {rho}]]$
  under the grading context $[[|D|]]$.
\end{definition}

Valid substitutions and semantic well-typedness both satisfy weakening
properties.
\begin{lemma}[Weakening for valid substitutions and
  semantic well-typedness]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok\_renaming}, \suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{weakening\_Sem}}
  \begin{mathpar}
    \mprset{fraction={===}}
    \infer[\ottdrulename{V\_weak}]{\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G ++ x : l A]]}}{\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}} \and
    \infer[\ottdrulename{SWt\_weak}]{
      \swt{[[G]]}{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[A]]}
      }{  \swt{[[G ++ x : l0 A]]}{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[A]]} }
  \end{mathpar}  
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate by unfolding the respective definitions of valid
  substitutions and semantic well-typedness.

Given a valid substitution map $[[rho]]$ from
$[[G]]$ to $[[D]]$, the validity of $[[rho]]$ does not necessarily
imply the well-formedness of the types in $[[G]]$. % The universal

Instead, the existence of interpretations
for types in $[[G]]$ is separately provided by the semantic
context well-formedness judgment, written \judgement{$\swf{[[G]]}$}, which we
inductively define as follows.
\begin{mathpar}
  \infer[\ottdrulename{SWf\_nil}]{~}{\swf{[[nil]]}} \and
  \infer[\ottdrulename{SWf\_cons}]{
    \swf{[[G]]} \\
    \swt{[[G]]}{[[A]]}{[[l]]}{[[Univ i]]} }{ \swf{[[G ++ x : l A]]}
  }
\end{mathpar}
The following lemma is useful when working with judgments of the form
$\swt{[[G]]}{[[A]]}[[l]][[Univ i]]$.
\begin{lemma}[Semantic type inversion]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{SemWt\_Univ}}
  The following are equivalent:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\swt{[[G]]}{[[A]]}{[[l]]}{[[Univ i]]}$;
    \item For every $\rho$, $[[D]]$ such that $\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$,
      \begin{itemize}
        \item $[[| D | |- A { rho } : l]]$ holds, and
        \item there exists $S$ such that $\interp{[[|D|]]}{[[i]]}{[[A { rho }]]}{S}$.
      \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate by the inversion lemma for types of the form $[[Univ i]]$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:interpinv}).
\end{proof}

We now specify the fundamental theorem and provide brief proof
sketches for the interesting cases.
\begin{theorem}[Fundamental theorem]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{soundness}}
  \label{thm:fundamental}
  If $[[G |- a : l A]]$ then $\swt{[[G]]}{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[A]]}$;
  if $[[|- G]]$ then $\swf{[[G]]}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By mutual induction over syntactic typing and
well-formedness. Despite the complex set-up of the logical predicate,
semantic typing, and their properties, the proof of the fundamental
theorem is straightforward and is similar to the proofs by logical
relation for simply typed languages such as the one found in
\citet{abel:poplmark}.

For example, the \rref*{Wt-App} case more or less proves itself since
the idea of valid input to valid output is baked into the
\rref*{SWt-Pi} case of the logical predicate.

For other cases involving eliminators such as \rref*{Wt-J} and \rref*{Wt-Let}, the
scrutinee either reduces to a desired normal form (e.g. $[[refl]]$ or $[[(l0 a, b)]]$) or a neutral
term. In the former case, the conclusion follows by the backward
closure property (\ref{lem:backclos}) and the induction
hypothesis. In the latter case, we know that the elimination form
evaluates to a neutral form and thus semantically inhabits the type by
adequacy (\ref{lem:adequacy}).

The \rref*{Wt-Conv} case is justified by
the preservation of meaning under expansion (\rref*{SWt-Par}),
reduction (\ref{lem:lrpreservation}), and indistinguishability (\ref{lem:lrfunci}).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Weak normalization for well-typed terms]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{normalization}}
  \label{cor:wtwn}
  If $[[G |- a : A]]$, then $[[wnf a]]$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate by the fundamental theorem (\ref{thm:fundamental}) and the
  admissible \rref{V-id}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Decidability of type conversion}
\label{sec:decconv}
In this section, we use the normalization theorem
(\ref{cor:wtwn}) to recover a decision procedure for type
conversion.

Let \judgement{$a \leadsto^{lo} b$} be the
leftmost-outermost reduction relation for
\name. 
% \begin{lemma}[Weak head factorization]
%   \label{lem:factorization}
%   If $[[a =>* b]]$ and $[[nf b]]$, then $[[a]]\leadsto^{h}[[b]]$.
% \end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}[Standardization]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{factorization.v}{standardization}}
  \label{lem:standardization}
  If $[[a =>* b]]$ and $[[nf b]]$, then $[[a]]\leadsto^{lo}[[b]]$.
\end{lemma}
Its proof, which we omit in the text,
is adapted from \citet{takahashi:par}'s standardization proof for the
untyped lambda calculus. We first prove Hindley's local postponement property~\citep{accattoli:factorization}
for the weak head reduction relation, and then obtain the
standardization theorem for leftmost-outermost reduction by iterating
weak head reduction. Similar to the mechanized standardization proof
found in \citet{mckinna:some-lambda-formalized}, we prove local
postponement for weak head reduction instead of head reduction since
the former admits a simpler specification.

%  Our proof makes a notable simplification to
% her original proof. Interested readers can refer to the
% \todo{remove?} appendix for details.

\begin{corollary}[Normalization is decidable]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{factorization.v}{LoRed\_normalize}}
  \label{lem:nomdec}
  If $[[wnf a]]$, then there exists an algorithm that computes its
  normal form.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  By standardization (\ref{lem:standardization}) and the fact that
  leftmost-outermost reduction is deterministic, we can repeatedly
  apply leftmost-outermost reduction to find the normal form for $[[a]]$.
\end{proof}


\begin{lemma}[Indistinguishability is decidable]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{IEq\_dec}}
  \label{lem:indistdec}
  If the lattice admits a decidable comparison operator, then indistinguishability
  is decidable. That is, given a grading context $[[E]]$, terms
  $[[A]]$ and $[[B]]$, and a level $[[l]]$, there is an algorithm that
  terminates with \texttt{true} or \texttt{false} depending on whether
  $[[E |- A == B @ l]]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  The algorithm, whose definition we omit, simply recurses over the
  structure of $[[A]]$.
  The correctness proof mirrors the definition of the algorithm and
  proceeds by induction on $[[A]]$. The variable case requires that the
  comparison $[[l0 <= l]]$ is decidable.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Algorithm for type conversion]
  Given a grading context $[[E]]$ and two well-formed types $[[A]]$
  and $[[B]]$, the algorithm decides their equality is as follows.
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item Run the decision procedure from \cref{lem:nomdec} on $[[A]]$ and
    $[[B]]$ to obtain normal forms $[[A0]]$ and $[[B0]]$.
  \item Recursivley compute $[[l0]]$ and $[[l1]]$, the minimal levels
    at which $[[A0]]$ and $[[B0]]$ can be graded respectively.
  \item Return the result of the decision procedure from
    \cref{lem:indistdec} on the relation
    $[[E |- A0 == A1 @ l0 \/ l1]]$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

In step (3),
the simulation lemma (\ref{lem:simulation}) justifies the use of
normal forms $[[A0]]$ and $[[B0]]$
before we check indistinguishability, whereas the downgrade
property (\rref*{I-down}) justifies the
use of the lowest possible level $[[l0 \/ l1]]$.

% The correctness of the algortihm
% follows from the syntactic properties about reduction,
% indistinguishability, and normal forms we have established in
% Sections~\ref{sec:synmeta} and \ref{sec:logpred}. One of the most
% notable properties is the admissible \rref*{I-Down} rule, which says
% choosing the lowest level is good enough.

The only missing piece is the decidability of the algorithm for
computing levels in step 2.
While conceptually simple, the algorithm unfortunately depends
on the structure of the lattice. For bottomless
lattices, there is no minimal level we can return for values such as
$[[Void]]$ and $[[Univ i]]$. Instead, we need to conjure a level
$[[l]]$ that can ignore as many boxed terms as possible when comparing
$[[A]]$ and $[[B]]$. There are sufficient conditions for coming up
with such a level, such as the existence of a bottom element,
or the size being (possibly infinitely) countable. The
precise condition is not easily specified.

As a result, we prove the decidability of a weaker version of the
algorithm where the level used to compare $[[A0]]$ and $[[B0]]$ is
given as an input. We formally state this property as follows.
\begin{lemma}[Decidability of indexed type conversion]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{iconv\_dec.v}{iconv\_dec}}
  \label{lem:deciconv}
  Given a context $[[E]]$, syntactically well-formed types $[[A]]$ and
  $[[B]]$, and a level $[[l]]$, the following statement is decidable.
  \[ \exists [[A0]][[B0]], [[A =>* A0]] \land [[B =>* B0]] \land [[E |- A0 == B0 @ l]] \]
\end{lemma}
Note that the statement that we have shown decidable corresponds
exactly to
the preconditions in \rref{E-Conv}. The type conversion rule
\rref*{Wt-Conv} is decidable as long as the lattice has a suitable
structure for computing some minimal level $[[l0]]$ for comparing the normalized
types through the indistinguishability judgment.

For finite or countable lattices, the decision procedure from
Lemma~\ref{lem:deciconv} can be iterated to recover the decidability
of conversion in \rref{Wt-Conv}.

\subsection{Scrutinee level constraints} \label{sec:scrutinee-level}

The level of scrutinees must be bounded by the level of the overall eliminator.
In particular, in \rref{Wt-J}, the level $[[l2]]$ of an equality proof $[[p]]$
must be bounded by that of its elimination $[[J c l2 p]]$,
and in \rref{Wt-Let}, the level $[[l1]]$ of a pair $[[a]]$
must be bounded by that of its elimination $[[let (l0 x, y) = l1 a in b]]$.
The bound is required to prove the \nameref{lem:simulation} property.
Suppose that scrutinees could instead be typed at any level.
First, the indistinguishability rules would need to be updated
so that scrutinees are indistinguishable if their level is higher
than that of the overall eliminator, just like in \rref{I-App}.
The modified premises are highlighted in \hlcolor{} below.
%
\begin{mathpar}
  \setlength{\fboxsep}{1.5pt}
  \infer[\ottdrulename{I\_J\_bad}]{
    \mathcolorbox{\hlcolor}{[[E |- p0 == p1 @ l l2]]} \\
    [[E |- c0 == c1 @ l]]
  }{
    [[E |- J c0 l2 p0 == J c1 l2 p1 @ l]]
  }
  \and
  \infer[\ottdrulename{I\_Let\_bad}]{
    \mathcolorbox{\hlcolor}{[[E |- a0 == a1 @ l l1]]} \\
    [[(# E ++ x : l0 #) ++ y : l1 |- b0 == b1 @ l]]
  }{
    [[E |- let (l0 x, y) = l1 a0 in b0 == let (l0 x, y) = l1 a1 in b1 @ l]]
  }
\end{mathpar}

As concrete examples, by \rref{I-J-bad},
supposing $[[J c H p]]$ were well typed in context $[[G]]$
at level $[[L]]$ with $[[G |- p : H a ~ L a]]$,
we can conclude that $[[| G | |- J c H refl == J c H p @ L]]$.
The problem is that the left-hand side reduces to $[[c]]$,
while the right-hand side does not necessarily reduce to something indistinguishable from $[[c]]$,
thereby violating simulation.
The same issue arises with pairs, where we may be able to conclude by \rref{I-Let-bad} that
$[[| G | |- let (H x, y) = H (H a, b) in c == let (H x, y) = H d in c @ L]]$,
and the left-hand side reduces to $[[c {a / x, b / y}]]$,
while the right-hand side again does not necessarily reduce to something indistinguishable from it.
Even if the context were empty, simulation still cannot be proven
without knowing \apriori that a closed proof of equality $[[p]]$ normalizes to $[[refl]]$,
or that a closed term of a pair type normalizes to a pair.

The one exception is the eliminator for the empty type,
whose scrutinee may be well typed at any arbitrary level in \rref{Wt-Absurd}.
Since $[[absurd b]]$ has no reduction rules beyond the congruence \rref{P-Absurd},
ignoring $[[b]]$ in \rref{I-Absurd} will not violate simulation.

\subsection{Equality type level constraints} \label{sec:eq-level}

In \rref{Wt-Eq}, the observer level $[[l0]]$ of the equality type $[[a ~ l0 b : A]]$
must be bounded by the level $[[l]]$ of the overall equality type.
Otherwise, \name would be inconsistent.
Suppose that $[[l0]]$ did not have to be bounded by $[[l]]$.
Once again, the indistinguishability rule would need to be modified
so that the endpoints are compared using guarded indistinguishability
to handle the situation where $[[l0]]$ is higher
than the observer level $[[l]]$ of the indistinguishability judgement.
The modified premises are highlighed in yellow below.
%
\begin{mathpar}
  \setlength{\fboxsep}{1.5pt}
  \infer[\ottdrulename{I\_Eq\_bad}]{
    \mathcolorbox{\hlcolor}{[[E |- a0 == a1 @ l l0]]} \\
    \mathcolorbox{\hlcolor}{[[E |- b0 == b1 @ l l0]]}
  }{
    [[E |- a0 ~ l0 b0 : A0 == a1 ~ l0 b1 : A1 @ l]]
  }
\end{mathpar}

By \rref{I-Eq-Bad}, it would be possible to derive the judgement
$[[nil |- Unit ~ H Unit : Univ i == Unit ~ H Void : Univ i @ L]]$
via \rref{GI-Indist} for both premises since $[[not (H <= L)]]$.
The left-hand size is inhabited by $[[refl]]$,
which would then also be a proof of the right-hand side via \rref{Wt-Conv},
a contradiction.
\nameref{thm:preservation} is also violated,
since by the contradiction $[[J (\ H x . x) L refl]]$ would be an inhabitant of $[[Void]]$,
while it reduces to $[[(\ H x . x)]]$, which is not.

\subsection{Scoped Logical Predicate,  non-Kripke Style}
\label{sec:nokripke}
% To justify the type conversion rule (\rref*{Wt-Conv}) semantically, we

The well-gradedness constraints in Figure~\ref{fig:logpred} introduces scoping information to our
logical predicate. As observed by \citet{abel:normalization}, to
derive the adequacy property for a scoped logical relation, the
interpretation for functions must be generalized to account for
weakened contexts. Logical relations augmented with this
generalization are referred to as Kripke-style logical relations and
can be found in \citet{abel:poplmark, abel:irrelevance,
  adjedj:mltt-coq, harper:equivalence}.

However, the logical predicate for \name is different in that the
scoping information is not the full-fledged typing judgment, unlike
the forementioned works, but the weaker well-gradedness judgment. This
allows us to introduce in Section~\ref{sec:logpred} an ill-typed but
well-graded constant $[[dummy]]$, which has similar reduction behavior
as free variables, but is not subject to scoping constraints.

When deriving the normalization of functions, we can use the antirenaming
property formulated in terms of $[[d]]$ to avoid weakening the grading
context with a Kripke-style logical predicate. This proof technique
simplifies our definition of the logical predicate significantly; in a
dependently typed system, a Kripke-style predicate would require us to
generalize not only functions, but also function and sigma types themselves
since they both involve binders.

\subsection{Towards typed definitional equality and relational

  \sigspace([[c]], [[l]], [[l0]], S_A, R) &\coloneqq
    \exists a, b, [[c =>* (l0 a, b)]] \wedge
    \member{x}{[[l0]]}{S_A} \wedge
    (\forall S_B, R(a, S_B) \to \member{b}{[[l]]}{S_B}) \\
  \univspace{j}([[A]], [[E]]) &\coloneqq \exists S, \interp{[[E]]}{[[j]]}{[[A]]}{S} \\
  \eqspace([[p]], [[l0]], [[a]], [[b]], [[E]]) &\coloneqq
    [[p =>* refl]] \wedge \exists a', b', [[a =>* a']] \wedge [[b =>* b']] \wedge [[E |- a == b @ l0]]
\end{align*}
