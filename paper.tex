\section{Introduction}

Dependency-tracking type systems govern how functions can depend on their
inputs. Essentially, these systems prevent the flow of information from
high-level sources to low-level results.

The \emph{\longname} (\dcoi)~\cite{liu:dcoi}
adapts dependency tracking to the context of a dependently-typed programming
language. The primary goal of dependency tracking in \dcoi is to identify
irrelevant arguments. These arguments can be erased during compilation to
produce faster execution and can be ignored during equivalence checking. A key
notion is \emph{indistinguishability}: a definition of program equivalence
that takes into account the parts of the program that an observer may depend
on. However, dependency-tracking type systems serve many purposes, such as
staged compilation and information flow control~\cite{abadi:dcc}.

\dcoi lays the foundation for the design of a dependently-typed
programming language based on dependency tracking. Its conversion
may use indistinguishability at any observer level when comparing types for
equality. Despite this coarse notion of equality, prior work showed that \dcoi
is type sound.

Prior work focused on syntactic methods for reasoning about
\dcoi. While sufficient for type soundness, they cannot be used to identify
an instantiation of \dcoi that enjoys properties such as logical consistency,
weak normalization, and decidable observer-indexed equivalence. For flexibility,
\dcoi is designed around a \emph{Pure Type System} (PTS), where various
instances of sorts, rules and axioms determine the versions of quantification
that is allowed in the language. While some instances of PTSes are known to
allow nontermination (such as when including the $[[Type]]:[[Type]]$ axiom),
others (such as those that include a predicative universe hierarchy, or a
single impredicative sort) can form the basis of a consistent logic.

In this paper, we define the language \name, which is a dependent type theory
featuring a predicative universe hierarchy. This calculus retains many of the
features of \dcoi, including level-indexed dependent function types
($[[Pi x : l A . B]]$), level-indexed dependent pairs ($[[Sig x : l A. B]]$),
level-indexed propositional equality $[[a ~l b]]$, and an empty type
$[[Void]]$. \jc{sentence here about what this list is about}

\section{\name}

In this section, we present the grammar and judgements of \name,
which is an instantiation of the \longname (\dcoi)~\citep{liu:dcoi} with
the Pure Type System (PTS)~\citep{barendregt:PTS} axioms and rules
of a predicative universe hierarchy.
The grammar is given in \cref{fig:grammar},
which is parameterized over a meet-semilattice of dependency levels
(following \citet{abadi:dcc}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{alignat*}{2}
  [[G]], [[D]] & \Coloneqq [[nil]] \mid [[G ++ x : l A]] & \quad & \text{Typing contexts with levels and types} \\
  [[E]], [[F]] & \Coloneqq [[nil]] \mid [[E ++ x : l]] & \quad & \text{Grading contexts with levels} \\
  [[A]], [[B]], [[C]], &&& \\
  [[a]], [[b]], [[c]], [[p]] & \Coloneqq & \quad & \text{Terms} \\
  & \mid [[x]] \mid [[Univ i]] \mid [[Void]] \mid [[absurd b]] & \quad & \textit{variables, type universes, empty type, absurd} \\
  & \mid [[Pi x : l A . B]] \mid [[\ l x . b]] \mid [[b l a]] & \quad & \textit{function types, abstractions, applications} \\
  & \mid [[Sig x : l A . B]] \mid [[(l a, b)]] \mid [[let (l0 x, y) = l a in b]] & \quad & \textit{pair types, pairs, matching} \\
  & \mid [[a ~ l b]] \mid [[refl]] \mid [[J c l p]] & \quad & \textit{equality types, reflexivity, J eliminator}
\end{alignat*}
\caption{Grammar of contexts and terms}
\label{fig:grammar}
\end{figure}

We use the metavariable $[[l]]$ for dependency levels,
and $[[l0 /\ l1]]$ for the meet of $[[l0]]$ and $[[l1]]$.
As in the previous section, we use the low $[[L]]$ and high $[[H]]$ levels in our examples,
with $[[L < H]]$.
Similarly, we use the metavariables $[[i]], [[j]], [[k]]$ for universe levels,
which we take to be meta-level naturals,
and $[[max i j]]$ for the maximum of $[[i]]$ and $[[j]]$.
Although our mechanization uses de Bruijn indices for representing binding
and simultaneous substitutions,
for clarity we use named variables and single substitutions for our presentation.
We use the metavariables $[[x]], [[y]], [[z]]$ for variables
and $[[a { b / x }]]$ for the substitution of a term $[[b]]$ in place of the variable $[[x]]$
inside the term $[[a]]$.

The main judgement is the typing judgement,
which has the form \judgement{$[[G |- a : l A]]$} for a term $[[a]]$
that is well type under context $[[G]]$ at observer level $[[l]]$
with type $[[A]]$.
To reduce clutter,
we use the judgement \judgement{$[[G |- a : A]]$} to mean that there exists some level $[[l]]$
such that $[[G |- a : l A]]$ holds.
The typing rules are defined mutually with the context well-formedness rules,
both given in \cref{fig:typing:dep}.
When viewed as a PTS, the \rref{Wt-Univ,Wt-Pi} correspond
to the sort axioms $([[Univ i]], [[Univ i + 1]])$ for every $[[i]]$
and the sort rules $([[Univ i]], [[Univ j]], [[Univ max i j]])$ for every $[[i]], [[j]]$.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \drules[Wf]{$[[ |- G]]$}{Context well-formedness}{Nil, Cons}
  \drules[Wt]{$[[G |- a : l A]]$}{Well-typedness}{Var,Univ,Empty,Absurd,Pi,Abs,App}
  \caption{Well-formedness and typing rules (universes, empty, functions)}
  \label{fig:typing:dep}
\end{figure}

Deferring our discussion of using levels for dependency tracking to \cref{sec:dep},
the remaining rules for variables, function abstractions, function applications,
the empty type, and its eliminator are standard for dependent type theory.
To change the type of a term to an equivalent one,
typing uses a definitional equality that is defined in terms of indistinguishability (\cref{sec:indist}).
We then look at the rules for dependent pair types (\cref{sec:pairs}),
and finally for propositional equality types (\cref{sec:eq}),
which internalize indistinguishability.

\subsection{Dependency tracking} \label{sec:dep}

The observer level $[[l]]$ of a typing judgement $[[G |- a : l A]]$
tracks what level variables $[[a]]$ may use, as well as the level at which $[[a]]$ may be used.
The former is enforced by the level constraint premise in \rref{Wt-Var},
which permits a variable to be used only if the observer level is at least as high as that of the variable.
Informally, higher-level information cannot leak into a lower-level term.

In \rref{Wt-Abs}, the function parameter is annotated with a fixed level,
thus restricting its uses to observers at least that high.
Even so, there is no restriction on that level,
and a function may be applied to an argument of a higher level,
as long as the annotated level of the function parameter
matches the annotated level of the application in \rref{Wt-App}.
Allowing a higher-level parameter is useful because it may be used in the function's \emph{type},
which is typed at an arbitrary level independent of the function itself
in the second premise of \rref{Wt-Abs}.
By \rref{Wt-Pi}, there are no restrictions on the levels of the domain and codomain types either
(as long as they match).

As an example, consider the following typing judgement,
using $[[l A -> B]]$ as shorthand for nondependent functions $[[Pi x : l A . B]]$
where $[[x]]$ does not appear in $[[B]]$.
%
\begin{displaymath}
  [[:concrete: nil |- (\ H X . \ H x . \ L f . f H x) : L (Pi X : H Univ 0 . H X -> L (H X -> X) -> X)]]
\end{displaymath}

The entire function itself is typed at level $[[L]]$,
while the parameters $[[X]]$ and $[[x]]$ are annotated at level $[[H]]$,
so the inner function body cannot be $[[x]]$,
for instance, even if it has the correct type.
On the other hand, the parameter $[[f]]$ is a function that takes a high-level $[[X]]$
and returns a low-level $[[X]]$, so $[[f H x]]$ is a valid function body.
Meanwhile, the type of the entire function itself need only be well-typed at \emph{some} level,
and in particular is well-typed at $[[H]]$,
which would allow using the high-level $[[X]]$
in the codomain of the type.

In general, we can prove a regularity lemma stating that the type of a well-typed term
is itself well typed at a level not necessarily related to that of the term,
and may even be higher, as is the case for the example above.
Intuitively, this is permitted because there is no way to retrieve the type of a term at run time,
so the type of a lower-level term can safely use higher-level information
without it leaking into the term.

\begin{lemma}[Regularity]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{Wt\_Wff}, \suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{Wt\_regularity}}
  \label{lem:regularity}
  If $[[G |- a : l A]]$, then $[[|- G]]$ holds,
  and there exist some $[[l0]], [[i]]$
  such that $[[G |- A : l0 Univ i]]$.
\end{lemma}

While generally a lower-level term may not meaningfully use higher-level information,
a proof of the empty type $[[Void]]$ at \emph{any} level
may be eliminated to \emph{any other} level via \rref{Wt-Absurd}.
By logical consistency, which we prove in \cref{sec:semmeta},
there is no closed term of type $[[Void]]$,
and so it cannot contain any meaningful information,
and furthermore represents a dead execution path.
From a type-theoretic perspective,
the unconstrained elimination level allows us to handle impossible cases of a lower-level term
using a higher-level proof of its impossibility.

\subsection{Definitional equality and indistinguishability} \label{sec:indist}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule{Wt-Conv}
    \drule{E-Conv}
    \drule{P-AppAbs}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Conversion, definitional equality, and parallel reduction ($\beta$ only) rules}
  \label{fig:conv}
\end{figure}

The typing rules also include \rref{Wt-Conv}, shown in \cref{fig:conv},
wich allows converting the type of a well-typed term to a definitionally equal type.
Our definitional equality is untyped, but eventually relies on the levels of variables,
so its judgement form \judgement{$[[E |- a == b]]$} requires a grading context $[[E]]$.
In \rref{Wt-Conv}, this is provided by the erasure operation \judgement{$[[| G |]]$},
which denotes dropping all type annotations to convert a typing context into a grading context.

Definitional equality is defined by \rref{E-Conv} in \cref{fig:conv},
which separately incorporates parallel reduction and indistinguishability.
For convenience, we define the shorthand \judgement{$[[E |- a <=> b @ l]]$}
as a level-annotated form of definitional equality:
it holds iff $[[a =>* c0]]$, $[[b =>* c1]]$, and $[[E |- c0 == c1 @ l]]$.
Consequently, $[[E |- a == b]]$ holds iff there exists some level $[[l]]$
such that $[[E |- a <=> b @ l]]$ holds.

Parallel reduction \judgement{$[[a => b]]$} consists of the $\beta$-reduction rules for our elimination forms,
listed in \cref{fig:conv} and in following subsections,
along with rules for the reflexive, congruent closure, omitted here.
Multi-step parallel reduction \judgement{$[[a =>* b]]$} is then the transitive closure of parallel reduction.
This formulation of equality is in contrast to prior work by \citet{liu:dcoi},
where parallel reduction is merely a metatheoretic tool for proving type soundness.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \drules[I]{$[[E |- a == b @ l]]$}{Indistinguishability}{Var,Univ,Empty,Absurd,Pi,Abs,App}
  \drules[GI]{$[[E |- a == b @ l l0]]$}{Guarded indistinguishability}{Dist,Indist}    
  \caption{(Guarded) indistinguishability rules (universes, empty, functions)}
  \label{fig:indist}
\end{figure}

Indistinguishability is mutually defined with guarded indistinguishability,
given in \cref{fig:indist}.
Guarded indistinguishability is used in \rref{I-App};
otherwise, two terms are indistinguishable at observer level $[[l]]$
if they have the same shape and their subterms are also indistinguishable at $[[l]]$.

We say that indistinguishability is \emph{coarser} than mere $\alpha$-equivalence
(and hence $[[E |- a == b]]$ is coarser than mere $\beta$-equivalence of $[[a]]$ and $[[b]]$)
because more terms may be indistinguishable while not necessarily being $\alpha$-equivalent.
This is because the use of guarded indistinguishability when comparing arguments in \rref{I-App},
which are labelled at $[[l0]]$, but observed at $[[l]]$.
If $[[l0 <= l]]$, meaning that the observer can fully see the arguments,
then \rref{GI-Dist} applies, and the applications are indistinguishable
if both the functions and the arguments are indistinguishable at $[[l]]$ respectively.
Otherwise, if $[[not (l0 <= l)]]$, then \rref{GI-Indist} applies,
and the arguments are too high to be observed at $[[l]]$,
so indistinguishability of the applications does not consider the arguments.
As a simple example,
$[[:concrete: (# w : L ++ x : H #) ++ y : H |- (\ H z . w) H x == (\ H z . w) H y @ L]]$
holds even though $[[x]]$ and $[[y]]$ could be anything,
because the observer level is $[[L]]$,
while the arguments' levels are $[[H]]$ and therefore unobservable.
Indistinguishability of unobservable arguments articulates the intuition that
functions may safely ignore these arguments that they cannot meaningfully use.

A level comparison also occurs in \rref{I-Var},
where a variable may only be indistinguishable from itself
if the observer level is high enough to observe it.
This prevents equating arbitrary terms via function applications, such as
$[[:concrete: x : H ++ y : H |- (\ H z . z) H x == (\ H z . z) H y]]$,
since the bodies of the functions are not indistinguishable.
Without this level comparison,
we have via \rref{E-Conv} the equalities $[[nil |- Unit == (\ H z . z) H Unit]]$,
$[[nil |- (\ H z . z) H Unit == (\ H z . z) H Void]]$,
and $[[nil |- (\ H z . z) H Void == Void]]$,
which would permit a closed term of $[[Void]]$ via repeated applications of \rref{Wt-Conv}.

An important property of indistinguishability is the simulation property,
which enforces that indistinguishable terms must reduce in lockstep.
This is crucial for proving further syntactic lemmas in \cref{sec:synmeta},
such as the preservation of definitional equality between two terms as they reduce.

\begin{lemma}[Simulation]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{simulation\_star}}
  \label{lem:simulation}
  If $[[E |- a0 == b0 @ l]]$ and $[[a0 =>* a1]]$,
  then there exists some $[[b1]]$ such that $[[b0 =>* b1]]$
  and $[[E |- a1 == b1 @ l]]$.
\end{lemma}

\subsection{Dependent pairs} \label{sec:pairs}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule[width=0.5\textwidth]{Wt-Sig}
    \drule[width=0.5\textwidth]{Wt-Pair} \\
    \drule[width=0.5\textwidth]{Wt-Let}
    \drule[width=0.5\textwidth]{P-LetPair}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Typing and parallel reduction rules (pairs)}
  \label{fig:typing:pair}
\end{figure}

Dependent pairs are annotated with a fixed level in their first component,
given by $[[l0]]$ in \rref{Wt-Sig,Wt-Pair} of \cref{fig:typing:pair}.
Like for function types, the levels of pair types are not necessarily related
to that annotated level.
While the first component of the pair constructor has a fixed level,
the level of the second component is the level of the overall pair.

In contrast to prior work by \citet{liu:dcoi},
rather than having pair projections,
pairs are eliminated using a pattern-matching expression
for greater expressivity.
The eliminator is typed according to \rref{Wt-Let},
and its reduction behaviour is given by \rref{P-LetPair}.
It targets a pair $[[a]]$ annotated at level $[[l1]]$,
binding its first and second components
as variables $[[x]]$ at level $[[l0]]$
and $[[y]]$ at level $[[l1]]$ respectively in the body $[[b]]$,
which is typed at the same level $[[l]]$ as the overall eliminator.
The overall type of the eliminator is specified by the motive $[[C]]$,
which is abstracted over the target $[[z]]$.
The level of the target $[[l2]]$ is not exactly $[[l]]$ but bounded above by it;
its justification is a subtle technical issue,
which we defer to \cref{sec:target-level}.

The first and second pair projections can be recovered using the eliminator as follows,
assuming the level of the pair $[[a]]$ to be $[[l1]]$.
%
\begin{align*}
  [[proj1 l0 a]] &\coloneqq [[let (l0 x, y) = l1 a in x]] &
  [[proj2 l0 a]] &\coloneqq [[let (l0 x, y) = l1 a in y]]
\end{align*}

The typing rules for the projections can be stated as admissible rules
with the same premises as the rules from prior work,
and are given in \cref{fig:typing:proj} as
\rref*{Wt-proj1}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{admissible.v}{T\_Proj1}}
and \rref*{Wt-proj2}\punctstack{.}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{admissible.v}{T\_Proj2}}
The first projection is well typed only if the first component's level
is bounded by the level of the whole pair:
we can only project an observable component.
The second projection is well typed only if the first projection
is well typed at the first component's level $[[l0]]$.
This is because the type of the second component
is dependent on the first component at level $[[l0]]$.
Equivalently, the first projection well-typedness premise
can be replaced by the level constraint $[[l0 <= l]]$,
using \rref*{Wt-proj1} to recover well-typedness.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \mprset{fraction={===}}
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule{Wt-projOne}
    \drule{Wt-projTwo}
    \drule{Wt-projTwoalt}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Admissible typing rules for pair projections}
  \label{fig:typing:proj}
\end{figure}

However, this is not the only way we can ensure the well-typedness of the second projection.
The alternate admissible \rref{Wt-proj2alt} only requires well-typedness of the pair
and not of the first projection.
Here, the type of the second projection matches on the pair
and then subsitutes in the first component inside of $[[B]]$,
rather than matching on the pair within the substitution in $[[B]]$.
The second projection is therefore well typed regardless
of the well-typedness of the first projection.
In particular, we can take the second projection of a lower-level pair
even if it contains a higher-level first component.
This was not possible when pair projections were the primitive operators on a pair
and not the eliminator we have, which is strictly more expressive.

The box type $[[T l0 A]]$ from \cref{sec:examples}
can be seen as a pair with meaningful information only in its first component,
of which we only ever take the first projection,
and can therefore be encoded as a unary tuple as follows.
%
\begin{align*}
  [[T l0 A]] &\coloneqq [[Sig x : l0 A . Unit]] &
  [[box l0 a]] &\coloneqq [[(l0 a, unit)]] &
  [[unbox l0 a]] &\coloneqq [[proj1 l0 a]]
\end{align*}

By these encodings and the typing rules for pairs,
we obtain the admissible typing rules for box types
(\rref*{Wt-T}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{admissible.v}{T\_T}}),
boxes (\rref*{Wt-Box}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{admissible.v}{T\_Box}}),
and unboxing (\rref*{Wt-Unbox}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{admissible.v}{T\_Unbox}})
in \cref{fig:typing:box}.
The level constraint in \rref*{Wt-Unbox} comes from the corresponding constraint in \rref{Wt-Let},
and intuitively says that the only values that can be unboxed are observable ones.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \mprset{fraction={===}}
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule{Wt-T}
    \drule{Wt-Box}
    \drule{Wt-Unbox}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Admissible typing rules for box types}
  \label{fig:typing:box}
\end{figure}

The primary use case of the box type is to be able to enclose higher-level terms
and to treat them as indistinguishable from one another by a lower-level observer.
That is, $[[E |- box H a == box H b @ L]]$ ought to hold regardless of $[[a]]$ and $[[b]]$.
The idea generalizes to pairs by using guarded indistinguishability
when comparing first components in \rref{I-Pair},
similar to how function arguments are compared.

The remaining indistinguishability rules for pair types and eliminators
are given in \cref{fig:conv:pair}.
The constraint $[[l1 <= l]]$ in \rref{I-Let} is required due to its presence in \rref{Wt-Let}.

\subsection{Propositional equality} \label{sec:eq}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule{Wt-Eq}
    \drule{Wt-Refl} \\
    \drule[width=0.7\textwidth]{Wt-J}
    \drule{P-JRefl}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Typing and parallel reduction rules (equality)}
  \label{fig:typing:eq}
\end{figure}

The propositional equality type internalizes the indistinguishability judgement,
enabling reasoning about indistinguishability within \name itself.
The typing rules for constructs related to the equality type are given in \cref{fig:typing:eq}.
An equality type $[[a ~ l0 b]]$ represents the proposition that $[[a]]$ and $[[b]]$
are indistinguishable by observers at level $[[l0]]$.
By \rref{Wt-Eq}, the endpoints must themselves be well typed at level $[[l0]]$,
since it only makes sense to talk about the indistinguishability of observable terms,
just as \rref{I-Var} only holds for observable variables.
The observer level $[[l0]]$ must also be bounded by the overall level $[[l]]$
to maintain logical consistency,
which we explain in detail in \cref{sec:eq-level}.
Its constructor is the usual reflexivity proof $[[refl]]$ for well-typed terms,
given by \rref{Wt-Refl}.

In \rref{Wt-J}, the eliminator $\ottkw{J}$ takes as target
an equality proof $[[p]]$ annotated at level $[[l2]]$,
along with a method $[[c]]$.
The target must be well typed as an equality between $[[a]]$ and $[[b]]$
of type $[[A]]$, observed at level $[[l0]]$.
The observer, so to speak, is the motive $[[C]]$,
abstracted over the right-hand size of the equality $[[x]]$
(the left-hand side being fixed at $[[a]]$)
and the equality proof itself $[[y]]$.
As the observer, the motive must be well typed at level $[[l0]]$.
The type of the method is the motive instantiated with $[[a]]$ and $[[refl]]$,
while the type of the fully-applied eliminator is the motive instantiated with $[[b]]$ and $[[p]]$.
The levels of $[[x]]$ and $[[y]]$ must therefore match the levels of $[[b]]$ and $[[p]]$, respectively.
Because by \rref{P-JRefl} the eliminator reduces on $[[refl]]$ to the method,
the level of the method must match the level $[[l]]$ of the overall eliminator.
The level of the target $[[l2]]$ is not exactly $[[l]]$ but is bounded above by it;
its justification is a subtle technical issue,
which we defer to \cref{sec:target-level}.

The other level constraint in \rref{Wt-J},
which allows the equality endpoints to be typed at a level $[[l1]]$
possibly lower than the equality observer level $[[l0]]$,
increases the expressivity of the eliminator,
since the motive $[[C]]$ is able to abstract over $[[x]]$ at the lower level $[[l1]]$.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule[width=\textwidth]{Wt-downgrade}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Admissible typing rules for propositional equality}
  \label{fig:typing:eq:lemmas}
\end{figure}

In particular, it allows us to show the admissible
\rref{Wt-downgrade}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{admissible.v}{T\_Down\_Alt}}
in \cref{fig:typing:eq:lemmas},
which permits lowering the observer level of an equality proof.
Here, the motive is $[[a ~ l0 x]]$,
which otherwise would not have been well typed
if the level of $[[x]]$ had to match the observer level $[[l1]]$
of the equality $[[p]]$ being eliminated.
Intuitively, this rule internalizes the idea that if two terms are indistinguishable at some higher level,
then they are also indistinguishable at a lower level,
since a lower-level indistinguishability is coarser than a higher-level one,
and treats more things as indistinguishable.

  \begin{gather*}
    \drule[width=0.3\textwidth]{I-Eq}

  \caption{Indistinguishability rules (equality)}
  \label{fig:conv:eq}
\end{figure}

The shape of the indistinguishability rules
for the propositional equality constructs in \cref{fig:conv:eq} are unsurprising:
\rref{I-Eq,I-Refl,I-J} assert indistinguishability of the terms
given indistinguishability of each pair of subterms.
% In particular, we have admissible \rref{I-Refl-alt}
% via \rref{I-Eq,Wt-Conv}.
% It states that if two terms are indistinguishable by some observer,
% then $[[refl]]$ is a proof of their propositional equality at that observer level,
% which is why we say that propositional equality internalizes indistinguishability.
%
What is trickier are the level constraints that appear in these rules.
The constraint $[[l2 <= l]]$ in \rref{I-J} is required due to its presence in \rref{Wt-J}.
The constraint $[[l0 <= l]]$ in \rref{I-Eq} is yet another subtle technical issue,
and we defer its explanation to \cref{sec:eq-level}.

\section{Syntactic metatheory} \label{sec:synmeta}

In this section, we develop the syntactic metatheory of \name,
ultimately culminating in subjection reduction,
\ie type preservation of typing with respect to parallel reduction.
The metatheory has been mechanized in Coq,
and the development can be found in \repo{} under the \texttt{proofs/theories} directory.
Definitions, lemmas, and theorems given here are annotated with a footnote
to the corresponding file and top-level name in the development.
Some lemmas are presented as admissible derivation rules of the corresponding judgement,
which are written with a double horizontal bar instead of a single bar.
Their corresponding mechanizations are annotated where mentioned in the prose.

\subsection{Well-gradedness}

As indistinguishability and definitional equality involve a grading context,
we require a well-gradedness judgement on terms to be able to prove some substitution lemmas.
The judgement specifies the dependency level that a term may be assigned.
In later sections, we will see that semantic typing and the logical relation
also need to carry around some well-gradedness information.
The rules for the judgement can be seen as the same as the typing rules,
but with all typing information stripped out, leaving only the levels,
and are listed in \cref{fig:grading}.

\subsection{Properties of grading and indistinguishability}

The well-gradedness and indistinguishability judgements
satisfy some standard properties,
listed as admissible rules in \cref{fig:grading:lemmas},
\cref{fig:defeq:lemmas}, and \cref{fig:indist:lemmas}, respectively.
\Rref*{Wg-weak}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{iok\_morphing}} and
\rref*{I-weak}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{ieq\_weakening\_mutual}}
permit weakening the context.
\Rref*{Wg-subst}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{iok\_subst}} and
\rref*{I-subst}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{ieq\_iok\_subst}}
permit substituting in a well-graded term of the appropriate level
in place of a variable of that level in the context.

Well-gradedness is subsumptive
(\rref*{Wg-sub}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{iok\_subsumption}}):
the level of a well-graded term may be raised.
Additionally, parallel reduction preserves well-gradedness
(\rref*{Wg-pres}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{iok\_preservation}}).

Indistinguishability is reflexive for well-graded terms
(\rref*{I-refl}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{iok\_ieq}}),
symmetric (\rref*{I-sym}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{ieq\_sym\_mutual}}),
and transitive (\rref*{I-trans}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{ieq\_trans\_heterogeneous}}).
Transitivity is slightly more general in its observer level:
given a chain of three indistinguishable terms at two different observer levels,
the end terms are indistinguishable at the meet of the levels.
This generalization is crucial for definitional equality,
as we will see shortly.
Proving transitivity relies on a downgrade property
(\rref*{I-down}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{ieq\_downgrade\_mutual}}),
where knowing that a term is indistinguishable from another at two different levels
permits concluding that it is also indistinguishable at the meet of the levels.

\subsection{Properties of parallel reduction and definitional equality}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \mprset{fraction={===}}
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule{P-subst}
    \drule{Ps-subst}
    \drule{P-refl}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Admissible rules for parallel reduction}
  \label{fig:pars:lemmas}
\end{figure}

Parallel reduction and its transitive closure admit the substitution lemmas
\rref*{P-subst}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Par\_cong}} and
\rref*{Ps-subst}\punctstack{,}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Pars\_cong\_star}} respectively.
An additional useful lemma is that single parallel reduction itself is already reflexive
(\rref*{P-refl}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Par\_refl}}).
These are given in \cref{fig:pars:lemmas}.

The key lemmas of parallel reduction are the diamond property and confluence,
which state that if a term reduces to two different terms,
those two terms themselves must converge back to the same term.
The proof uses the notion of complete development%
\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{tstar}}
by \citet{takahashi:par}.

\begin{lemma}[Diamond]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Par\_confluent}}
  \label{lem:diamond}
  If $[[a => b0]]$ and $[[a => b1]]$, then
  there exists some $[[c]]$ such that $[[b0 => c]]$ and $[[b1 => c]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Confluence]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{Pars\_confluent}}
  \label{lem:confluence}
  If $[[a =>* b0]]$ and $[[a =>* b1]]$, then
  there exists some $[[c]]$ such that $[[b0 =>* c]]$ and $[[b1 =>* c]]$.
\end{lemma}

Confluence is required to show that
reduction of definitionally equal terms preserves definitional equality,
since definitional equality is defined in \rref{E-Conv}
as the indistinguishability of the two terms after parallel reduction.
The proof relies on the \nameref{lem:simulation} property of indistinguishability
to reconnect the two sides after reduction.

\begin{lemma}[Preservation of definitional equality]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{par.v}{iconv\_par2}}
  Suppose $[[E |- a0 == b0]]$.
  If $[[a0 => a1]]$ and $[[b0 => b1]]$,
  then $[[E |- a1 == b1]]$.
\end{lemma}

As with indistinguishability,
definitional equality admits weakening
(\rref*{E-weak}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_renaming}}),
and is reflexive for well-graded terms
(\rref*{E-refl}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_refl}}),
symmetric (\rref*{E-sym}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_sym}}),
and transitive (\rref*{E-trans}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_trans}}),
stated in \cref{fig:defeq:lemmas}.
Inverting the premises of transitivity,
each of them independently have some level at which two terms are indistinguishable,
so combining them requires the more general transitivity of \rref*{I-trans}.
The proof also relies on simulation.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \mprset{fraction={===}}
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule{E-weak}
    \drule{E-refl}
    \drule{E-sym}
    \drule{E-trans}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Admissible rules for definitional equality}
  \label{fig:defeq:lemmas}
\end{figure}

Finally, we will need some inversion lemmas for definitional equality on our types
(\ie injectivity of type constructors).

\begin{lemma}[Injectivity of function types]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_pi\_inj}}
  If $[[E |- Pi x : l0 A0 . B0 == Pi x : l1 A1 . B1]]$
  then $[[l0]] = [[l1]]$, $[[E |- A0 == A1]]$,
  and $[[E ++ x : l0 |- B0 == B1]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Injectivity of pair types]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_sig\_inj}}
  If $[[E |- Sig x : l0 A0 . B0 == Sig x : l1 A1 . B1]]$
  then $[[l0]] = [[l1]]$, $[[E |- A0 == A1]]$,
  and $[[E ++ x : l0 |- B0 == B1]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Injectivity of equality types]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_eq\_inj}}
  If $[[E |- a0 ~ l0 b0 == a1 ~ l1 b1]]$
  then $[[l0]] = [[l1]]$,
  and there exists an $[[l >= l0]]$
  such that $[[E |- a0 <=> a1 @ l]]$ and $[[E |- b0 <=> b1 @ l]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Injectivity of universes]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{conv.v}{conv\_univ\_inj}}
  If $[[E |- Univ i == Univ j]]$ then $[[i]] = [[j]]$.
\end{lemma}

\subsection{Type preservation}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \mprset{fraction={===}}
  \begin{gather*}
    \drule[width=0.2\textwidth]{Wt-weak}
    \drule{Wt-subst}
    \drule[width=0.2\textwidth]{Wt-sub}
    \drule[width=0.2\textwidth]{Wt-replace}
  \end{gather*}
  \caption{Admissible rules for well-typedness}
  \label{fig:typing:lemmas}
\end{figure}

As usual, typing admits weakening
(\rref*{Wt-weak}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{weakening\_Syn}})
and substitution
(\rref*{Wt-subst}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{subst\_Syn}}),
as stated in \cref{fig:typing:lemmas}.
Like grading, it also admits subsumption
(\rref*{Wt-sub}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{subsumption}}),
allowing the level to be raised while retaining the same type.
Furthermore, it satisfies replacement
(\rref*{Wt-replace}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{preservation\_helper}}),
allowing a type in the context to be replaced by a definitionally equal one.

Due to \rref{Wt-Conv},
deducing the well-typedness of subterms of a well-typed term
doesn't follow immediately from inversion and requires separate inversion lemmas,
which are standard and omitted here.
One notable consequence is that if $[[refl]]$ is a propositional equality
between $[[a]]$ and $[[b]]$ at some observer level $[[l0]]$,
then they are indistinguishable at that level.
Finally, we are able to prove type preservation,
which proceeds by induction on the typing derivation.

\begin{lemma}
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{Wt\_Refl\_Coherent}}
  If $[[G |- refl : l a ~ l0 b]]$,
  then there exist $[[a']], [[b']]$ such that
  $[[a =>* a']]$, $[[b =>* b']]$,
  and $[[| G | |- a' == b' @ l0]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[Type preservation]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{subject\_reduction},\suppl{theories}{preservation.v}{subject\_reduction\_star}}
  \label{thm:preservation}
  If $[[a => b]]$ and $[[G |- a : l A]]$
  then $[[G |- b : l A]]$,
  and similarly for $[[a =>* b]]$.
\end{theorem}

      j < i
    }{
      \interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[Univ j]]}{\buildset{[[A]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- A : l]] \wedge \univspace{j}([[E]], [[A]])}}
    }
    \and
    \infer[\ottdrulename{SWt\_Empty}]{~}{
      \interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[Void]]}{\buildset{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- a : l]] \wedge [[wne a]]}}
    }
    \and
    \infer[\ottdrulename{SWt\_Eq}]{~}{
      \interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[a ~ l0 b]]}{\buildset{[[p]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- p : l]] \wedge (\eqspace([[p]], [[l0]], [[a]], [[b]], [[E]]) \vee [[wne p]])}}
    }
    \and
    \infer[\ottdrulename{SWt\_Step}]{
      [[A => B]] \\
      \interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[B]]}{S}
    }{
      \interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}
    }
  \end{mathpar}  
  \caption{Logical predicate}
  \label{fig:logpred}
\end{figure}

In this section, we use a logical predicate to show weak
normalization and consistency for \name.
We structure the section as follows. In \cref{sec:logpred}, we
present the definition of the logical predicate and its properties. In
\cref{sec:soundness}, we define valid substitutions and
semantic typing judgments based on the logical predicate and then
prove the fundamental theorem, which states that all syntactically
well-typed terms must be semantically well-typed, from which we derive
weak normalization and consistency. In \cref{sec:decconv}, we combine
weak normalization and the standardization theorem to recover a decidable
type conversion algorithm.

\subsection{A logical predicate for \name}
\label{sec:logpred}
To characterize semantically well-behaved terms through the logical
predicate, we need to first define the notion of neutral and normal terms.
% Before we can define the logical predicate, we need to first define
% the notion of neutral and normal terms so we can characterize
% semantically well-behaved terms.
% Before we can state and prove the adequacy property for the
% logical predicate, we need to first define normal and neutral forms
% and prove some useful lemmas about how they interact with parallel
% reduction.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \drules[Ne]{$[[ne a]]$}{Neutral forms}{Var,App,Absurd,J,Let}
  \drules[Nf]{$[[nf a]]$}{Normal forms}{Pi,Abs,Univ,Void,Eq,Refl,Sig,Pair}
  \caption{Neutral and normal forms}
  \label{fig:normalform}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:normalform} defines neutral and normal forms in the
form of two judgments \judgement{$[[ne a]]$} and \judgement{$[[nf a]]$}.
These predicates characterize a set of terms that are free of redexes.
This property can be formulated using parallel reduction as follows.

\begin{lemma}
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{nf\_refl}}
  If $[[nf a]]$ or $[[ne a]]$, then for all $[[b]]$ such that $[[a =>
  b]]$, we have $[[a]] = [[b]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}[Weakly normalizing terms]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{wn}, \suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{wne}}
  Given a term $[[a]]$, if there exists some term $[[b]]$ such that
  $[[a =>* b]]$, then we say that $[[a]]$ weakly normalizes to a
  normal (resp. neutral) form, which we denote in judgment form as
  \judgement{$[[wnf a]]$} (resp. \judgement{$[[wne a]]$}).
\end{definition}

\Cref{fig:logpred} gives the full definition of our logical
predicate.
Similar to \citet{abel:irrelevance}'s semantic interpretation of types, 
we define our logical predicate, which takes the form
\judgement{$\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$}, as an inductive relation
recursively defined over the universe level $[[i]]$. The
metavariable $S$ represents a family of terms indexed by dependency levels. 
We use \judgement{$\member{a}{[[l]]}{S}$} to indicate that $[[a]]$ is
a term that belongs to the set in $[[S]]$ that is indexed by $[[l]]$.
When constructing indexed sets, we use
\judgement{$\buildset{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{\ldots}$} to indicate a family of sets
parameterized by $[[l]]$.
The judgment $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ states that under
grading context $[[E]]$ and universe level $[[i]]$, the type $[[A]]$
is interpreted as the family of sets $[[S]]$.

Unlike logical relations for simply typed languages, the logical
predicate for \name cannot be defined as a total function since not
all terms can be assigned an interpretation. Instead, the logical predicate
$\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ simultaneously specifies the
semantically well-formed types $[[A]]$ and their interpretation $[[S]]$.
% A key result we will present later in this section is the functionality of
% the logical predicate; that is, each term can only correspond to one
% interpretation $[[S]]$ if there exists one.

For concision, we introduce the notion of semantic inhabitance to
avoid mentioning the side condition that a type is well-formed when we
want to talk about whether a term inhabits the set obtained from
interpreting the type.

\begin{definition}[Semantic inhabitance]
  Given a context $[[E]]$ and a level $[[l]]$, 
  we say that $[[a]]$ \emph{semantically inhabits} the type $[[A]]$
  at level $[[l]]$ if there exists some $[[i]]$ and $[[S]]$ such that
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $\member{a}{[[l]]}{S}$.
\end{definition}

In \rref{SWt-Pi,SWt-Sigma}, the
metavariable $R$ represents a relation between terms and the
indexed family of terms $S$. Ignoring the conclusion of the rules, 
\rref*{SWt-Pi} and \rref*{SWt-Sigma} share the same preconditions,
which ensure their well-formedness in the following sense:
for every term $[[a]]$ that semantically inhabits the type $[[A]]$ at
level $[[l0]]$, the type $[[B {a / x}]]$ is well-formed.
% The interpreted sets appearing in the conclusion follows our usual
% intuition. 
In the function case, the definition of $\prodspace$
specifies the semantic inhabitants of a function type as the set of
terms that maps well-behaved terms from the input type to a well-behaved
output type.

The logical predicate takes in a grading context $[[E]]$ as a
parameter to ensure that $[[S]]$ only includes terms that are
well-graded with respect to the indexed level.
This property is
formally stated as follows and can be proven by straightforward
induction over the logical predicate.

\begin{lemma}[Escape]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUniv\_Ok}}
  \label{lem:escape}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $\member{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{S}$,
  then $[[E |- a : l]]$.
\end{lemma}

We refer to \cref{lem:escape} as the escape lemma as it is
reminiscent of the escape lemmas by \citet{abel:irrelevance} and
\citet{adjedj:mltt-coq}, though instead of escaping from the
logical predicate to the syntactic typing judgment, we only require
the much weaker well-gradedness judgment. The well-gradedness
restriction will be necessary for us to show that indistinguishable
types have the same interpretation.

Similar to its syntactic counterpart, the logical predicate satisfies
the subsumption property.

\begin{lemma}[Subsumption for the logical predicate]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_subsumption}}
  Suppose $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
  If $[[l0 <= l1]]$ and $\member{[[a]]}{[[l0]]}{S}$,
  then $\member{[[a]]}{[[l1]]}{S}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Immediate by induction over the derivation and \rref{Wg-sub}.
\end{proof}

Next, we show some properties which will be useful in
\cref{sec:soundness} for the proof of the fundamental
theorem. We omit some uses of the syntactic lemmas from
\cref{sec:synmeta} about reduction and indistinguishability
when presenting the proof sketches.
% The logical predicate is specified as an inductive relation, but we
% can further show that it is in fact a partial function; that is, given
% a grading context $[[E]]$, every type $[[A]]$ can only correspond to

The logical predicate satisfies the following inversion principles. 
These inversion principles are not immediately obtainable by inverting
the derivation because we close our interpretation with parallel
reduction, a full
reduction relation, in \rref{SWt-Step}. For brevity, we show only the cases
for equality, function, and empty types.

\begin{lemma}[Inversion of the logical predicate (selected)]
  \label{lem:interpinv}\leavevmode
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item \stack{~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_Eq\_inv}}
    If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[a ~ l0 b]]}{S}$, then $S =
    \buildset{[[p]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- p : l]] \wedge (\eqspace([[p]],
      [[l0]], [[a]], [[b]], [[E]]) \vee [[wne p]])}$.
  \item \stack{~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_Fun\_inv}}
    If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[Pi x : l0 A . B]]}{S}$, then there
    exist $S_A$ and $R$ such that following hold:
    \begin{itemize}
    \item $S = \buildset{[[b]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- b : l]] \wedge \prodspace([[b]], [[l]], [[l0]], S_A, R)}$;
    \item $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_A}$;
    \item $\forall a, \member{a}{[[l0]]}{S_A} \to \exists S_B, R(a,
      S_B)$; and
    \item $\forall a, S_B, \member{a}{[[l0]]}{S_A} \to R(a, S_B) \to
      \interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[B {a / x}]]}{S_B}$.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \stack{~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_Void\_inv}}
    If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[Void]]}{S}$, then $S =
    \buildset{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[E |- a : l]] \wedge [[wne a]]}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Immediate by induction over the derivation of the logical
  predicate.
\end{proof}

The set of terms that semantically inhabit a type are closed by
expansion (i.e. backward reduction).

\begin{lemma}[Backward closure]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_back\_clos}}
  \label{lem:backclos}
  Suppose $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $[[E |- a : l]]$.
  If $[[a => b]]$ and $\member{[[b]]}{[[l]]}{S}$,
  then $\member{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{S}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  By induction over the derivation of the logical predicate. The
  \rref*{SWt-Univ} case requires \rref*{SWt-Step}, the backward
  closure property baked into the definition of the logical
  predicate. All other cases are trivial.
\end{proof}

The logical predicate satisfies cumulativity: if a type has an
interpretation at a lower level, then it must also have the same
interpretation at a higher level.

\begin{lemma}[Cumulativity]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_cumulative}}
  \label{lem:cumulativity}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $[[i]] \leq [[j]]$, then
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[j]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  By induction on the derivation of $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$
  and the transitivity of $\leq$ for universe levels.
\end{proof}

\Rref{SWt-Step} closes the interpretation of types under
expansion. We can also show that the interpretation of types is
preserved under forward reduction through the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}[Reduction preserves interpretation]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_preservation}}
\label{lem:lrpreservation}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$ and $[[A => B]]$, then
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  By induction over the derivation of
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
  \Rref{SWt-Step} is the only interesting case, where we
  leverage the \nameref{lem:diamond} property of
  parallel reduction to reconcile two potentially distinct reductions
  from the type $[[A]]$.
\end{proof}

Next, we prove that the logical predicate is functional. That is,
given a context $[[E]]$ and a universe level $[[i]]$, a type $[[A]]$
can correspond to at most one interpretation $[[S]]$.

\begin{lemma}[Functionality]
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_deterministic}}
\label{lem:lrfunc}
If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_0}$ and
$\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_1}$, then $S_0 = S_1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  By induction over the derivation of
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_0}$.
  The \rref*{SWt-Step} case is immediate by \cref{lem:lrpreservation}.
  For all other cases, the conclusion
  follows by applying the inversion
  properties (\cref{lem:interpinv}) on
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_1}$.
\end{proof}

The logical predicate is functional not only for types that are
syntactically equal, but also for types that are
indistinguishable. This property is needed to fully justify the
soundness of the type conversion rule, since the type conversion
involves both reduction and indistinguishability.

\begin{lemma}[Functionality for indistinguishable types]
  \label{lem:lrfunci}
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{InterpUnivN\_IEq}}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_0}$,
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[B]]}{S_1}$, and
  $[[E |- A == B @ l ]]$ for some $[[l]]$,
  then $S_0 = S_1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  The argument is similar to the one for \cref{lem:lrfunc}.
  We start by induction over the derivation of
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S_0}$.
  For case \rref*{SWt-Step}, we need both \cref{lem:lrpreservation}
  and \nameref{lem:simulation}.
  For other cases, we apply the inversion properties on
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[B]]}{S_1}$.

  The cases for function and pair types both require one extra step to complete the
  proof. Consider the case where $[[A]] = [[Pi x : l0 A0 . B0]]$ and
  $[[B]] = [[Pi x : l0 A1 . B1]]$. The
  induction hypothesis says that for every term $[[a]]$ that
  semantically inhabits $[[A0]]$, if a type $[[C]]$ is
  indistinguishable from $[[B0 {a / x}]]$, then $[[C]]$ and $[[B0 {a /
  x}]]$ have the same meaning. To complete the proof, we use
  the induction hypothesis and instantiate the variable
  $[[C]]$ with the type $[[B1 { a / x}]]$ and prove that $[[B0 {a /
    x}]]$ is indistinguishable from $[[B1 {a / x}]]$.  From the
  premise that $[[Pi x : l0 A0 . B0]]$ and $[[Pi x : l0 A1 . B1]]$ are
  indistinguishable, we already know that $[[B0]]$ and $[[B1]]$ are
  indistinguishable.

  To prove that $[[B0 {a / x}]]$ and $[[B1 {a / x}]]$ are
  indistinguishable, we apply
  the substitution property of indistinguishability
  (\rref*{I-subst}).
  This requires $[[a]]$ to be well-graded at $[[l0]]$,
  which follows from $[[a]]$ semantically inhabiting $[[A0]]$ via \nameref{lem:escape}.
\end{proof}

which establishes the connection between the inhabitants of
the logical predicate and terms that weakly normalize to normal or
neutral forms.

Before we state and prove adequacy, we first show some
useful facts about normal forms. Following \citet{geuvers:cc-sn}, we
introduce a dummy constant $[[dummy]]$ to the set of
terms for presentation purposes. The dummy term satisfies the rules
specified in \cref{fig:dummy}.

Reductions and normal forms are preserved when we undo the substitutions
involving $[[dummy]]$.

\begin{lemma}[Antirenaming for Par]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{Par\_antirenaming}}
  \label{lem:paranti}
  If $[[a {dummy / x} => b]]$, then there exists some term $[[b0]]$
  such that $[[b]] = [[b0 {dummy / x}]]$ and $[[a => b0]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Antirenaming for normal and neutral forms]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{ne\_nf\_renaming\_with\_d}}
  \label{lem:nfneanti}
  If $[[nf a {dummy / x}]]$ (resp. $[[ne a {dummy / x}]]$), then $[[nf
  a]]$ (resp. $[[ne a]]$).
\end{lemma}

From \cref{lem:paranti,lem:nfneanti}, we derive the
following corollary about weak normalization.

\begin{lemma}[Antirenaming for weak normalization]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{normalform.v}{wn\_antirenaming}}
  \label{lem:wnanti}
  If $[[wnf a {dummy / x}]]$, then $[[wnf a]]$.
\end{lemma}

% Lemmas~\ref{lem:paranti}, \ref{lem:nfneanti}, and \ref{lem:paranti}
% would still hold if we replace the $[[dummy]]$ occurrences with
% arbitrarily chosen variables. However, the constant $[[dummy]]$ is
% different from variables as it is well-graded on all grading contexts,
% whereas variables are subject to scoping restrictions.

% For \name, we need to include scoping information into the definition of

In \citet{geuvers:cc-sn}, the $[[dummy]]$ constant can be defined as
syntactic sugar for a fresh variable. However, this is not possible in \name
because we want \rref{I-D,Wg-D} to hold. When the grading context is empty,
there is no variable we are allowed to pick. However, since
\rref{I-Absurd} does not impose any scope checking on the target of
the empty eliminator, we can define $[[dummy]]$ as $[[absurd x]]$ for
an arbitrarily picked variable $[[x]]$. It is easy to verify that this
definition respects the rules in \cref{fig:dummy} and the
antirenaming lemmas.

Finally, we state and prove the adequacy lemma.

\begin{lemma}[Adequacy]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{semtyping.v}{adequacy}}
  \label{lem:adequacy}
  If $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$, then the following hold:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item If $\member{a}{[[l]]}{S}$, then $[[wnf a]]$;
  \item If $[[E |- a : l]]$ and $[[wne a]]$, then $\member{a}{[[l]]}{S}$; and
  \item $[[wnf A]]$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  By induction over the derivation of
  $\interp{[[E]]}{[[i]]}{[[A]]}{S}$.
  The only interesting part of the proof is showing normalization for
  well-formed function and pair
  types. We focus on function types, and the proof for pair types is
  almost identical.

  In the function case, we have $[[A]] = [[Pi x : l0 A0 . B0]]$. By
  the induction hypothesis, we already know $[[wnf A0]]$ and for all
  terms $[[a]]$ that semantically inhabit $[[A0]]$, the term $[[B0 {a
    / x}]]$ is weakly normalizing. To show that $[[Pi x : l0 A0. B0]]$
  is weakly normalizing, it suffices to show that $[[B0]]$ is weakly
  normalizing. 

  Since the induction hypothesis for $[[B0]]$ is universally
  quantified, we need to find a concrete term to instantiate $[[a]]$
  with. By the induction hypothesis for $[[A0]]$, we also know that
  if $[[E |- a : l]]$ and $[[a]]$ is a neutral term, then $[[a]]$
  semantically inhabits $[[A0]]$. By \rref{I-D}, we pick $[[dummy]]$
  for $[[a]]$ and deduce that $[[B0 {dummy / x}]]$ is weakly
  normalizing. By \cref{lem:wnanti}, we conclude that $[[B0]]$ is
  also weakly normalizing.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Soundness}
\label{sec:soundness}
To define semantic well-typedness, we need to first define the notion
of valid substitutions, also referred to as valuations.
While we continue to use named variables rather than de Bruijn indices,
in this section we frame substitution in terms of \emph{substitution maps}
(or simply \emph{substitutions} for short)
and simultaneous substitutions.

\begin{definition}[Substitution maps]
  A substitution map $\rho$ is a mapping from variables to terms.
  We use $\dom(\rho)$~to denote the variables in the domain of $\rho$,
  $\rho(x)$~the term to which $\rho$ maps $[[x]]$,
  $[[nil]]$~the identity substitution map,
  $[[(rho ++ { a / x })]]$~the extension of $[[rho]]$
  by a mapping from $[[x]]$ to $[[a]]$,
  and $[[a { rho }]]$~the simultaneous substitution
  of the variables $x \in \dom(\rho)$ in $[[a]]$ by $\rho(x)$.
\end{definition}
The singleton substitution map $[[{ a / x }]]$ is then defined as $[[(nil ++ { a / x })]]$,
the single extension of the identity substitution map.

\begin{definition}[Valid substitutions]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok}}
  \label{def:semwf}
  A substitution map is a valid substitution 
  from $[[G]]$ to $[[D]]$,
  written \judgement{$\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$},
  if for every $[[x : l A in G]]$,
  $[[| D | |- rho(x) : l]]$ holds,
  and for all $i$, $S$ such that
  $\interp{[[|D|]]}{[[i]]}{[[A { rho }]]}{S}$, 
  we have $\member{\rho(x)}{[[l]]}{S}$.
\end{definition}

By definition, every term $[[rho( x)]]$ in a valid substitution map
$[[rho]]$ from $[[G]]$ to $[[D]]$ is well-graded with respect to the erased context
$[[|D|]]$. This allows us to derive the following lemma about
well-gradedness and valid substitutions.

\begin{lemma}[Valid substitutions for well-graded terms]
\stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{iok\_$\rho$\_ok\_morphing}}
  If $[[|G| |- a : l]]$ and $\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$, then $[[|D| |-
  a{rho} : l ]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Immediate by iterating the substitution principle for well-gradedness (\rref*{Wg-subst}).
\end{proof}
% The $[[| D | |- rho(x) : l]]$ requirement in the definition may appear
% redundant but is in fact required; we are unable to recover it with
% Lemma~\ref{lem:escape} since the definition of valid substitution does
% not imply the existence of interpretations for types in the context.
Valid substitutions satisfy the following admissible structural rules.

\begin{lemma}[Structural rules for valid substitutions]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok\_id}, \suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok\_cons}}
  \label{lem:vsubst}
  \leavevmode
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \label{lem:vnil} For every $[[G]]$, $\sws{[[G]]}{[[nil]]}{[[G]]}$ holds; and
    \item \label{lem:vcons} Given $\sws{[[D]]}{[[rho]]}{[[G]]}$,
      if $[[a]]$ semantically inhabits $[[A { rho }]]$
      at level $[[l0]]$ under grading context $[[| D |]]$,
      then $\sws{[[D]]}{[[rho ++ {a / x} ]]}{[[G ++ x : l0 A ]]}$ holds.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  (\ref{lem:vnil}) is easily justified by the part of the adequacy lemma
  that states well-graded neutral terms (variables in $[[G]]$ in
  this case) semantically inhabit any well-formed types.

  (\ref{lem:vcons}) requires \nameref{lem:lrfunc} and
  \nameref{lem:cumulativity} to convert the fact that
  $\member{[[a]]}{[[l0]]}{S}$ for the specific $S$ from the
  semantic inhabitance to the universal statement that
  $\member{[[a]]}{[[l0]]}{S_0}$ for arbitrary $j$ and $S_0$ such that
  $\interp{[[|D|]]}{[[j]]}{[[A { rho }]]}{S_0}$, as needed by the
  definition of valid substitutions.
\end{proof}

% The dependency on the functionality property in the proof of \rref{V-cons} makes 
% it tempting to simply replace the universal quantifier in the definition
% of valid substitutions with an existential quantifier. This change
% would allow us to omit the well-gradedness side condition for each
% $[[rho(x)]]$ since we can now derive it from the escape lemma

Next, we define semantic typing based on the definitions of valid
substitutions and the logical predicate.

\begin{definition}[Semantic well-typedness]
  \label{def:semwt}
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{SemWt}}
  A term $[[a]]$ is semantically well typed with type $[[A]]$
  at level $[[l]]$ under context $[[G]]$,
  written \judgement{$\swt{[[G]]}{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[A]]}$},
  if for every $\rho$, $[[D]]$,
  given $\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$,
  the term $[[a {rho}]]$ semantically inhabits the type $[[A {rho}]]$
  under the grading context $[[|D|]]$.
\end{definition}

Valid substitutions and semantic well-typedness both satisfy weakening
properties.

\begin{lemma}[Weakening for valid substitutions and
  semantic well-typedness]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok\_renaming}, \suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{weakening\_Sem}}
  \leavevmode
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \stack{~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{$\rho$\_ok\_renaming}}
      If $\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G ++ x : l A]]}$, then $\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$.
    \item \stack{~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{weakening\_Sem}}
      If $\swt{[[G]]}{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[A]]}$,
      then $\swt{[[G ++ x : l0 A]]}{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[A]]}$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Immediate by unfolding the respective definitions of valid
  substitutions and semantic well-typedness.
\end{proof}
The weakening property aligns with the intuitive understanding that a
substitution map for a context $[[G]]$ also closes over any
prefixes of $[[G]]$.

The following lemma is useful when working with judgments of the form
$\swt{[[G]]}{[[A]]}[[l]][[Univ i]]$.

\begin{lemma}[Semantic type inversion]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{SemWt\_Univ}}
  The following are equivalent:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\swt{[[G]]}{[[A]]}{[[l]]}{[[Univ i]]}$;
    \item For every $\rho$, $[[D]]$ such that $\sws{[[D]]}{\rho}{[[G]]}$ holds,
      \begin{itemize}
        \item $[[| D | |- A { rho } : l]]$ holds; and
        \item There exists an interpretation $S$ such that $\interp{[[|D|]]}{[[i]]}{[[A { rho }]]}{S}$.
      \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate by the inversion lemma for types of the form $[[Univ i]]$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:interpinv}).
\end{proof}

We now specify the fundamental theorem and provide proof
sketches for the interesting cases.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental theorem]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{soundness}}
  \label{thm:fundamental}
  If $[[G |- a : l A]]$ then $\swt{[[G]]}{[[a]]}{[[l]]}{[[A]]}$;
  if $[[|- G]]$ then $\swf{[[G]]}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By mutual induction over syntactic typing and
well-formedness. Despite the complex setup of the logical predicate,
semantic typing, and their properties, the proof of the fundamental
theorem is straightforward and is similar to the proofs by logical
relation for simply typed languages such as the one by
\citet{abel:poplmark}.

For example, the \rref*{Wt-App} case more or less proves itself since
the idea of valid inputs to valid outputs is baked into the
\rref*{SWt-Pi} case of the logical predicate.

For other cases involving eliminators such as \rref*{Wt-J} and \rref*{Wt-Let}, the
target either reduces to a desired normal form (\eg $[[refl]]$ or $[[(l0 a, b)]]$) or a neutral
term. In the former case, the conclusion follows by
\nameref{lem:backclos} and the induction
hypothesis. In the latter case, we know that the elimination form
evaluates to a neutral form and thus semantically inhabits the type by
\nameref{lem:adequacy}.

The \rref*{Wt-Conv} case is justified by
the preservation of meaning under expansion (\rref*{SWt-Par}),
reduction (\cref{lem:lrpreservation}), and indistinguishability (\cref{lem:lrfunci}).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Weak normalization for well-typed terms]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{soundness.v}{normalization}}
  \label{cor:wtwn}
  If $[[G |- a : A]]$, then $[[wnf a]]$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  Immediate by the \nameref{thm:fundamental} and \cref{lem:vsubst} (\ref{lem:vnil}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Standardization]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{factorization.v}{standardization}}
  \label{lem:standardization}
  If $[[a =>* b]]$ and $[[nf b]]$, then $[[a]]\leadsto^{lo}[[b]]$.
\end{lemma}

Its proof, which we omit in the text,
is adapted from the standardization proof for the untyped lambda calculus by \citet{takahashi:par}.
We first prove Hindley's local postponement property~\citep{accattoli:factorization}
for the weak head reduction relation, and then obtain the
standardization theorem for leftmost-outermost reduction by iterating
weak head reduction. Similar to the mechanized standardization proof
found in \citet{mckinna:some-lambda-formalized}, we prove local
postponement for weak head reduction instead of head reduction since
the former admits a simpler specification.

\begin{corollary}[Normalization is decidable]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{factorization.v}{LoRed\_normalize}}
  \label{lem:nomdec}
  If $[[wnf a]]$, then there exists an algorithm that computes its
  normal form.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  By \nameref{lem:standardization} and the fact that
  leftmost-outermost reduction is deterministic, we can repeatedly
  apply leftmost-outermost reduction to find the normal form for $[[a]]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Indistinguishability is decidable]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{geq.v}{IEq\_dec}}
  \label{lem:indistdec}
  If the lattice admits a decidable comparison operator, then indistinguishability
  is decidable. That is, given a grading context $[[E]]$, terms
  $[[A]]$ and $[[B]]$, and a level $[[l]]$, there is an algorithm that
  terminates with \texttt{true} or \texttt{false} depending on whether
  $[[E |- A == B @ l]]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  The algorithm, whose definition we omit, simply recurs over the
  structure of $[[A]]$.
  The correctness proof mirrors the definition of the algorithm and
  proceeds by induction on $[[A]]$. The variable case requires that the
  comparison $[[l0 <= l]]$ be decidable.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Algorithm for type conversion]
  Given a grading context $[[E]]$ and two well-formed types $[[A]]$
  and $[[B]]$, the algorithm deciding their equality is as follows.
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item Run the algorithm from \cref{lem:nomdec} on $[[A]]$ and
    $[[B]]$ to obtain normal forms $[[A0]]$ and $[[B0]]$.
  \item Compute the minimal levels $[[l0]]$ and $[[l1]]$
    at which $[[A0]]$ and $[[B0]]$ can be graded respectively.
  \item Return the result of the decision procedure from
    \cref{lem:indistdec} on the relation
    $[[E |- A0 == A1 @ l0 \/ l1]]$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

In step (3), \nameref{lem:simulation} justifies the use of
normal forms $[[A0]]$ and $[[B0]]$
before we check indistinguishability, while downgrade
(\rref*{I-down}) justifies the
use of the lowest possible level $[[l0 \/ l1]]$.

% The correctness of the algortihm
% follows from the syntactic properties about reduction,
% indistinguishability, and normal forms we have established in
% Sections~\ref{sec:synmeta} and \ref{sec:logpred}. One of the most
% notable properties is the admissible \rref*{I-Down} rule, which says
% choosing the lowest level is good enough.

The only missing piece is the decidability of the algorithm for
computing levels in step 2.
While conceptually simple, the algorithm depends
on the structure of the lattice. For bottomless
lattices, there is no minimal level we can return for values such as
$[[Void]]$ and $[[Univ i]]$. Instead, we need to conjure a level
$[[l]]$ that can ignore as many boxed terms as possible when comparing
$[[A]]$ and $[[B]]$. There are sufficient conditions for coming up
with such a level, such as the existence of a bottom element,
or the size of the lattice being (possibly infinitely) countable. The
precise condition is not easily specified.

As a result, we prove the decidability of a weaker version of the
algorithm where the level used to compare $[[A0]]$ and $[[B0]]$ is
given as an input. We formally state this property as follows.

\begin{lemma}[Decidability of indexed type conversion]
  \stack{.~}\footnote{\suppl{theories}{iconv\_dec.v}{iconv\_dec}}
  \label{lem:deciconv}
  Given a context $[[E]]$, syntactically well-formed types $[[A]]$ and
  $[[B]]$, and a level $[[l]]$, $[[E |- A0 <=> B0 @ l]]$ is decidable.
\end{lemma}

Note that $[[E |- A0 <=> B0 @ l]]$ corresponds
exactly to the preconditions of \rref{E-Conv}. The type conversion rule
\rref*{Wt-Conv} is decidable as long as the lattice has a suitable
structure for computing some minimal level $[[l0]]$
for deciding indistinguishability of the normalized types.

For finite or countable lattices, the decision procedure from
\cref{lem:deciconv} can be iterated to recover the decidability
of conversion in \rref{Wt-Conv}.

\subsection{Target level constraints} \label{sec:target-level}

The level of eliminator targets must be bounded by the level of the overall eliminator.
In particular, in \rref{Wt-J}, the level $[[l2]]$ of an equality proof $[[p]]$
must be bounded by that of its elimination $[[J c l2 p]]$,
and in \rref{Wt-Let}, the level $[[l1]]$ of a pair $[[a]]$
must be bounded by that of its elimination $[[let (l0 x, y) = l1 a in b]]$.
The bound is required to prove the \nameref{lem:simulation} property.
Suppose that targets could instead be typed at any level.
First, the indistinguishability rules would need to be updated
so that targets are indistinguishable if their level is higher
than that of the overall eliminator, just like in \rref{I-App}.
The modified premises are highlighted in \hlcolor{} below.
%
\begin{mathpar}
  \setlength{\fboxsep}{1.5pt}
  \infer[\ottdrulename{I\_J\_bad}]{
    \mathcolorbox{\hlcolor}{[[E |- p0 == p1 @ l l2]]} \\
    [[E |- c0 == c1 @ l]]
  }{
    [[E |- J c0 l2 p0 == J c1 l2 p1 @ l]]
  }
  \and
  \infer[\ottdrulename{I\_Let\_bad}]{
    \mathcolorbox{\hlcolor}{[[E |- a0 == a1 @ l l1]]} \\
    [[(# E ++ x : l0 #) ++ y : l1 |- b0 == b1 @ l]]
  }{
    [[E |- let (l0 x, y) = l1 a0 in b0 == let (l0 x, y) = l1 a1 in b1 @ l]]
  }
\end{mathpar}

As concrete examples, by \rref{I-J-bad},
supposing $[[J c H p]]$ were well typed in context $[[G]]$
at level $[[L]]$ with $[[G |- p : H a ~ L a]]$,
we can conclude that $[[| G | |- J c H refl == J c H p @ L]]$.
The problem is that the left-hand side reduces to $[[c]]$,
while the right-hand side does not necessarily reduce to something indistinguishable from $[[c]]$,
thereby violating simulation.
The same issue arises with pairs, where we may be able to conclude by \rref{I-Let-bad} that
$[[| G | |- let (H x, y) = H (H a, b) in c == let (H x, y) = H d in c @ L]]$,
and the left-hand side reduces to $[[c {a / x, b / y}]]$,
while the right-hand side again does not necessarily reduce to something indistinguishable from it.
Even if the context were empty, simulation still cannot be proven
without knowing \apriori that a closed proof of equality $[[p]]$ normalizes to $[[refl]]$,
or that a closed term of a pair type normalizes to a pair.

The one exception is the eliminator for the empty type,
whose target may be well typed at any arbitrary level in \rref{Wt-Absurd}.
Since $[[absurd b]]$ has no reduction rules beyond the congruence \rref{P-Absurd},
ignoring $[[b]]$ in \rref{I-Absurd} will not violate simulation.

It may seem desirable, then, to simplify things and enforce the target's level
to be exactly the overall level $[[l]]$,
but this prevents us from proving subsumption (\rref*{Wt-sub}),
since the level of the target appears in the context.
In both \rref{Wt-J,Wt-Let}, this occurs when type checking the motive $[[C]]$,
which abstracts over the target.
Supposing that both the level of the target and the overall level are $[[l]]$,
subsumption states that given a derivation at level $[[l]]$,
we need to construct a derivation at a higher level $[[l']]$.
In the case of these eliminators, we are given a derivation concluding something of the shape
$[[G ++ x : l poltergeist |- C : l0 Univ i]]$,
but we require one of the shape
$[[G ++ x : l' poltergeist |- C : l0 Univ i]]$,
which we are unable to conclude.

\subsection{Equality type level constraints} \label{sec:eq-level}

In \rref{Wt-Eq}, the observer level $[[l0]]$ of the equality type $[[a ~ l0 b]]$
must be bounded by the level $[[l]]$ of the overall equality type.
Otherwise, \name would be inconsistent.
Suppose that $[[l0]]$ did not have to be bounded by $[[l]]$.
Once again, the indistinguishability rule would need to be modified
so that the endpoints are compared using guarded indistinguishability
to handle the situation where $[[l0]]$ is higher
than the observer level $[[l]]$ of the indistinguishability judgement.
The modified premises are highlighed in yellow below.
%
\begin{mathpar}
  \setlength{\fboxsep}{1.5pt}
  \infer[\ottdrulename{I\_Eq\_bad}]{
    \mathcolorbox{\hlcolor}{[[E |- a0 == a1 @ l l0]]} \\
    \mathcolorbox{\hlcolor}{[[E |- b0 == b1 @ l l0]]}
  }{
    [[E |- a0 ~ l0 b0 == a1 ~ l0 b1 @ l]]
  }
\end{mathpar}

By \rref{I-Eq-Bad}, it would be possible to derive the judgement
$[[nil |- Unit ~ H Unit == Unit ~ H Void @ L]]$
via \rref{GI-Indist} for both premises since $[[not (H <= L)]]$.
The left-hand size is inhabited by $[[refl]]$,
which would then also be a proof of the right-hand side via \rref{Wt-Conv},
a contradiction.
\nameref{thm:preservation} is also violated,
since by the contradiction $[[J (\ H x . x) L refl]]$ would be an inhabitant of $[[Void]]$,
while it reduces to $[[(\ H x . x)]]$, which is not.

Because the observer level $[[l0]]$ is explicitly annotated in the equality type,
we cannot enforce $[[l]]$ to be exactly $[[l0]]$,
as it would violate subsumption by fixing the overall level to $[[l0]]$ in \rref{Wt-Eq}.

context with a Kripke-style logical predicate. This proof technique
simplifies our definition of the logical predicate significantly; in a
dependently typed system, a Kripke-style predicate would require us to
generalize not only functions, but also function and sigma types themselves
since they both involve binders.

\subsection{Towards typed definitional equality and relational
  semantic models}
\label{sec:typeddefeq}
Following \citet{liu:dcoi}, we formulate \name as an instantiation of
Barendregt's Pure Type Systems~\citep{barendregt:PTS} with a predicative
universe hierarchy.

A variation of PTS, referred to as Pure Type System with Judgmental
Equality by \citet{adams:pure}, replaces the untyped conversion rule from PTS
with a type-directed equality judgment. While untyped conversion rules
are still used in practice by theorem provers such as Coq~\citep{coq},
judgmental equality enables more expressive $\eta$ laws that cannot be
expressed without type annotation.

\citet{siles:PTS} proves the equivalence between typed and untyped
Pure Type Systems in the absence of $\eta$ laws.
One may wonder if a
similar equivalence holds between \name and a variant with typed
equality.
However, it turns out that even defining judgmental equality for \name
poses enough of a challenge. Consider the following
typed congruence rule for functions obtained by naively adding type
annotations to \rref{I-App}.
\begin{center}
  \drule{I-AppTy}
\end{center}
Ignoring the levels, \rref{I-AppTy} is exactly how the application
equivalence rule is defined for PTS with judgmental equality. However,
\rref{I-AppTy} breaks the
type correctness property, which states that $[[G |- a == b @ l : A]]$
implies $[[G |- a : l A]]$ and $[[G |- b : l A]]$.

When $[[not (l0 <= l)]]$, the function
arguments are not observable and therefore we do not need to check the
equality between $[[a0]]$ and $[[a1]]$ other than their respective
well-typedness. However, \name does not prohibit the type $[[B]]$ from
depending on the variable $[[x]]$ as the type $[[Pi x : l0 A . B]]$
can be well-formed at a level unrelated to the observer level $[[l]]$
(\ref{lem:regularity}).
As a result, there is no guarantee that $[[B {a0 / x}]]$ and $[[B {a1
  / x}]]$ are indistinguishable types.

As a simple counterexample, we can pick $[[b0]]$ and $[[b1]]$ to be
a level $[[L]]$ polymorphic identity function with a level $[[H]]$
(thus irrelevant)
type parameter. Instantiating $[[a0]]$ and $[[a1]]$ with distinct
types, we end up with two distinct arrow types $[[B {a0 / x}]] = [[L
a0 -> a0]]$ and $[[B {a1 / x}]] = [[L a1 -> a1]]$. Type correctness
breaks as soon as we add input type annotations to functions.

The problem of extending \name with judgmental equality also
directly relates to the problem of defining a relational model for justifying properties
such as extensionality, or a generalized semantic notion of noninterference
that goes beyond our syntactic simulation property
(\ref{lem:simulation}). Since logical relations are indexed by types,
when defining the interpretation for functions, we run into the same
issue where runtime irrelevant arguments that are vacuously related by guarded
equality may result in distinguishable types after being substituted
into the output type.

Rather than patching \rref{I-AppTy} with side conditions until
syntactic properties such as type correctness is satisfied, a more
pressing problem is whether \rref{I-AppTy} is semantically sound.
The counterexample about polymorphic identity function from earlier
shows that $[[b0 l0 a0]]$ and $[[b1 l0 a1]]$ do not necessarily
\emph{syntactically} inhabit both $[[B {a0 / x}]]$ $[[B {a1 / x}]]$. However,
semantically, two instantiated identity functions with different type
annotations do not behave differently at runtime.

In fact, we find it difficult, if not impossible, to construct a
counterexample where $[[b0 l0 a0]]$ and $[[b1 l0 a1]]$ do not behave
like terms from both $[[B {a0 / x}]]$ and $[[B {a1 / x}]]$.
To be more adversarial, we want a counterexample where the output type
$[[B]]$ pattern matches on the variable $[[x]]$ such that $[[B
{a0 /x}]]$ and $[[B {a1 / x}]]$ are types with different
elimination forms. If $[[b0 l0 a0]]$ and $[[b1 l0 a1]]$ evaluate to
different head forms, then we can break semantic soundness. However,
knowing that $[[b0 l0 a0]]$ and $[[b1 l0 a1]]$ are indistinguishable
at $[[l]]$ already rules out the case where they have different head
forms.

Therefore, despite the technical issues demonstrated in this section,
we are optimistic that our logical predicate can be further extended to
a relational model to justify more complex properties. 

% To solve the problem on the syntactic side, it is tempting to add a side
% condition to \rref{I-AppTy} to ensure the
% the indistinguishability between $[[B {a0 / x}]]$ and $[[B{a1 / x}]]$,

\section{Related work}

\subsection{Dependent Types and Irrelevance}
In this section, we discuss dependently typed systems with relevance
tracking. In the literature, irrelevance sometimes refers specifically
to proof-irrelevance or irrelevance during type conversion. Here, we
use a more liberal notion of relevance tracking that incorporates both
runtime erasability and proof irrelevance.
% both
% runtime erasability and proof-irrelevance and sometimes refer to .

% One specific use case of dependency tracking in dependently typed
% languages is relevance tracking. 

The Implicit Calculus of Constructions (ICC)~\citep{miquel:icc} and
its decidable variant ICC*~\citep{barras:icc} extend the Extended
Calculus
of Constructions~\citep{luo1990:extended} with an implicit quantifier that
is intended for irrelevant function arguments. A syntactic check is
performed to ensure that an irrelevant binder is free in the function
body after erasure. However, the type formation rules of both ICC and
ICC* permit the function's codomain type to treat an irrelevant binder
as relevant. This permissive design allows them to treat type
variables appearing in parametric polymorphism as irrelevant while
still ensure the well-formedness of types, which may depend on
those type variables. In \name, the same flexibility is achieved by
decoupling the observer level used to type check terms and types (\nameref{lem:regularity}); a
type variable can be irrelevant relative to a term's observer level
while still be relevant relative to its type's observer level.
Both ICC and ICC* use untyped $\beta$-equivalence as definitional equality
and therefore do not leverage erasure during type conversion.


\citet{pfenning:modal} introduces a modal type theory where function types
and typing contexts are annotated relevance levels. In his system,
there are separate judgment forms $\Gamma \vdash a : A $ and
$\Gamma \vdash a \div A$, which represent typing for \textit{terms}
and \textit{proofs} respectively. Pfenning's type system is
not parameterized by levels and only relevant variables can be used.
The judgment for proofs
(i.e. irrelevant terms)
$\Gamma \vdash a \div
A$ is defined as a synonym for $\Gamma^\oplus \vdash a : A $, where
$\Gamma^\oplus$ is obtained by changing all relevance
annotations in $\Gamma$ to be relevant. The operation that resets the
relevance levels in the context is referred to as \emph{resurrection} and
allows terms in irrelevant positions to use variables that are
formerly marked as irrelevant.

\citet{abel:irrelevance} extend Pfenning's system with a predicative
universe hierarchy. They prove the consistency and decidability of a
typed-directed conversion algorithm for their extended system through a
Kripke-style logical relation. Similar to \citet{pfenning:modal}, the
system by Abel and Scherer satisfies the property which they refer to
as soundness of \emph{internal erasure}, meaning that erasure of
irrelevant terms can be carried out for open terms without sacrificing
consistency or decidability. 
\name also enjoys the internal erasure
property. In fact, the consistency of \name relies on the soundness of
internal erasure since type conversion is defined using the
indistinguishability judgment.

While \citet{abel:irrelevance} uses Pfenning's resurrection mechanism
for internal erasure, the Erasure Pure Type Systems (EPTS) by
\citet{mishra:erasure} uses the mechanism only for runtime
erasure. Like ICC, the EPTS uses untyped $\beta$
equivalence as its definitional equality; relevance levels do not
affect type conversion.

Unlike in ICC, ICC*, EPTS, and \name, an irrelevant binder in \citet{pfenning:modal,
  abel:irrelevance}'s systems must be treated as
irrelevant in both a function's body and its output type. As a result,
a runtime erasable type variable must be marked as relevant if the
variable appears relevantly in the type signature. One such example is
the type variable from the polymorphic identity
function. \citet{abel:irrelevance} observe that allowing
runtime irrelevant but compiletime relevant binders
makes it difficult to define a
type-directed checking algorithm, an issue that is side-stepped by our
use of an untyped conversion rule. In Section~\ref{sec:typeddefeq}, we
observe a technical issue related to extending \name with typed
equality, though we formulate this issue in the simpler context of a
declarative judgmental equality.

In the Predicative Calculus of Cumulative Inductive
Construction (PCUIC)~\citep{sozeau:coq-coq-correct}, a simplified core
language for Coq~\citep{coq}, the proof-irrelevant \texttt{Prop} sort can be used
for erasure at runtime. Given a type $A$ with sort \texttt{Prop}, the
inhabitants of $A$ can be safely erased from the terms during program
extraction. The soundness of erasure for closed terms (referred to as
\emph{external} or \emph{post-mortem erasure} by
\citet{abel:irrelevance}) is ensured by a form of dependency tracking,
where only singleton types (e.g. equality proofs) or empty
types of the \texttt{Prop} sort can be destructed to produce runtime
relevant terms whose types are of the relevant sort \texttt{Type}.

The \texttt{Prop} sort in PCUIC does not affect type conversion and
thus does not admit internal erasure. Erasing terms with sort
\texttt{Prop} during type conversion could cause type
conversion to diverge; with a variable of type $A = A \rightarrow A$
in the context, it is possible to construct a term that erases to the
diverging lambda term $(\lambda x . x\ x)\ (\lambda x . x\ x)$.% ~\citep{casinghino:combining-proofs-programs}

\citet{gilbert:sprop} introduce the language sMLTT, an extension to
MLTT with a proof irrelevant sort named \texttt{SProp}. Unlike the
\texttt{Prop} sort, inhabitants of some type $A:$ \texttt{SProp} are
treated as definitionally equal during type conversion. They show the
consistency of their design for both predicative and impredicative
variants of \texttt{SProp} through a syntactic translation to the
Extensional Type Theory. Similar to \name, their base system only
allows the empty type to be eliminated from \texttt{SProp} to the
relevant universes. An optional extension to their system also allows
an equality type that inhabits \texttt{SProp} to be eliminated
to produce a relevant expression. However, their decidability proof,
based on \citet{abel:irrelevance}, does not cover the equality
extension. In the Agda and Coq implementations of \texttt{SProp},
neither support elimination from non-empty strict propositions to the
relevant sort. In the context of observational equality,
\citet{pujet:ttobs, pujet:ccobs} allow the elimination of observational equalities
from the proof-irrelevant universe to build relevant terms and manage
to prove the decidability of type conversion.

\citet{mishra:erasure} refers to the use of sorts to
mark irrelevance as an intrinsic treatment of irrelevance as the
relevance of a term is determined by its type. An extrinsic treatment
of irrelevance, on the other hand, determines relevance purely based
on the context in which the term is used. \name and other systems such
as EPTS, ICC, and the system by \citet{abel:irrelevance} can all be
viewed systems that treat relevance
extrinsically. \citet{mishra:erasure} argue that the extrinsic
approach addresses code duplication problems as it avoids the need to
duplicate data type and function definitions for both relevant and
irrelevant sorts.
